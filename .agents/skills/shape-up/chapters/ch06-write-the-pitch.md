# Chapter 6: Write the Pitch

## Core Idea
A pitch turns private shaping into a decision-ready potential bet. It gives stakeholders enough context to judge the problem, appetite, solution, risk, and limits without turning the concept into a fixed specification.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Five pitch ingredients**: **Problem**, **Appetite**, **Solution**, **Rabbit holes**, and **No Gos**.
  - When to use: Use all five when a shaped concept is ready for the betting table.
  - How: Present a specific customer story, state the time budget, show the core elements, patch risky details, and name excluded behavior.
- **Problem and solution together**: A solution needs a problem as its test of fitness.
  - When to use: Use this rule whenever discussion jumps to a feature or interface.
  - How: Describe one concrete story that shows why the current state fails, then test the proposed solution against it.
- **Appetite as a constraint**: The pitch asks what solution fits the time worth spending, not what the unlimited solution could include.
  - When to use: Use it to stop expensive alternatives from dominating the discussion.
  - How: State “two weeks” or “six weeks” beside the solution.
- **Embedded sketches**: Add one level of visual detail when readers need to see a linchpin concept.
  - When to use: Use it when a breadboard leaves too much to imagine.
  - How: Add a fat-marker sketch or a clean annotated sketch, and state that designers may change incidental layout.
- **Asynchronous presentation**: Post the pitch for private reading before a real-time decision.
  - When to use: Use it to protect focus and keep the betting table short.
  - How: Let comments find holes and add information. Make the yes-or-no decision at the betting table.

## Key Concepts
- **Pitch**: A document that presents a shaped project idea for consideration.
- **No Go**: Functionality or a use case intentionally excluded from the concept.
- **Linchpin**: A part of the concept that readers must see concretely to understand the whole.
- **Baseline story**: A specific account of the current problem used to judge the solution.
- **Unshaped work**: A problem without a solution that is ready for a team.

## Mental Models
- Think of a pitch as a **presentation of a bet**, not a requirements document.
- Use a **specific story** as a test case for both demand and solution fitness.
- Use **selective concreteness**. Add detail only where readers need it to understand the concept.

## Anti-patterns
- **Solution without problem**: The discussion becomes an endless debate about attractive alternatives.
- **Problem without solution**: The team must research and invent under a fixed deadline.
- **High-fidelity pitch**: Wireframes pull the betting discussion into color, layout, and other details.
- **Comment-driven commitment**: Comments may improve a pitch, but they do not replace the betting decision.

## Worked Example
A Payment Form pitch called out a rabbit hole: URLs would not use custom domains in version one. It also named a no-go: users would not get WYSIWYG form editing. They could upload a logo and set header text on a separate customize page. These limits kept the solution inside the appetite. Other Basecamp pitches used screenshots or videos to show the problem, then fat-marker sketches and breadboards to show the solution.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pair every solution with a concrete problem.
2. State the appetite as part of the problem constraint.
3. Do not pitch a problem that still lacks a shaped solution.
4. Call out rabbit holes and no-gos before the bet.
5. Use asynchronous reading, then decide at the betting table.
