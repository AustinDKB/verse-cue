# Chapter 13: Show Progress

## Core Idea
Task counts and estimates hide uncertainty. The Hill Chart shows whether each scope is still being figured out or has reached the known, executable side of the hill.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The hill**: Work has an uphill phase of solving the approach and a downhill phase of executing known work.
  - When to use: Use it for status when percentages and estimates mislead.
  - How: Place each scope on a spectrum from unknown, through known, to done.
- **Hill Chart**: A visual history of scope positions and movement.
  - When to use: Use it so managers can see status without interrupting the team.
  - How: Team members move scope dots, save updates, and add context when needed.
- **No one says “I don’t know”**: The chart makes uncertainty visible without requiring a person to announce failure.
  - When to use: Use it when social pressure makes teams hide stuck work.
  - How: Treat a dot that does not move as a prompt to ask what unknown blocks it.
- **Build your way uphill**: Thinking of an approach is only the first part of solving it.
  - When to use: Use it when scopes backslide after a premature “solved” decision.
  - How: Separate “I thought about this,” “I validated my approach,” and “I built enough to expose remaining unknowns.”
- **Solve in the right sequence**: Push the most important and uncertain scopes uphill first.
  - When to use: Use it at project start and when choosing the next scope.
  - How: Solve scary work before routine screw-tightening.
- **Inverted pyramid**: Put essential, uncertain work first and leave routine details for last.

## Key Concepts
- **Uphill**: A scope still contains unknowns or unsolved problems.
- **Downhill**: A scope has no meaningful unknowns left and needs execution.
- **Unknown**: A question whose answer may change the implementation or design.
- **Second-order view**: Seeing how work moves over time, not only its current location.
- **Backsliding**: Moving a scope back uphill after a false sense of certainty.

## Mental Models
- Think of a task as a **hill climb**. The top means knowing what to do, not finishing every action.
- Use the Hill Chart as a **conversation filter**. Ask about the stuck scope, not every task.
- Treat novel risk like the top of an **inverted pyramid**. Solve it before low-risk polish.

## Anti-patterns
- **Task-count status**: A list with no tasks may mean done or undiscovered work.
- **Numerical certainty**: “Four hours” cannot express a familiar task versus a novel task with a three-day risk.
- **Head-only solving**: An imagined approach may fail when code or design meets reality.
- **Routine-first sequencing**: It leaves the hardest unknown until the deadline.

## Worked Example
Planning a dinner begins uphill. Choosing a cuisine moves partway up, selecting an Indian recipe and preparing a shopping list reaches the top, and shopping and cooking move downhill. In a product project, a “Notify” scope looked stuck because email design, back-end delivery, and in-app display were bundled together. Splitting those into separate scopes let each dot show its true position.

## Key Takeaways
1. Track unknowns and solved approaches, not task totals.
2. Move scope dots with the team's full context.
3. Use a motionless dot as a raised hand.
4. Split a scope when its parts move independently.
5. Solve the riskiest important work before routine work.
