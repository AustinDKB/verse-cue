# Chapter 2: Principles of Shaping

## Core Idea
Good shaped work stays at the right level of abstraction. It gives a team a clear solution and clear limits while leaving room for expert judgment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rough**: Shaped work looks unfinished and leaves open spaces for the designer and programmers who will build it.
  - When to use: Keep work rough during shaping when later decisions need team expertise.
  - How: Show the main idea without committing to every visual, task, or implementation detail.
- **Solved**: Shaped work connects the main elements of the solution and removes visible rabbit holes.
  - When to use: Require this before a project reaches the betting table.
  - How: Spell out the macro solution, key flows, and known tricky decisions.
- **Bounded**: Shaped work states the appetite and what the team will not do.
  - When to use: Use boundaries to stop scope from expanding during the cycle.
  - How: Define the time box, the core use case, and explicit exclusions.
- **Two tracks**: Shaping stays private and runs ahead of the building track.
  - When to use: Use this separation when unshaped work is too risky to schedule.
  - How: Shape possible future projects during a cycle while teams build earlier bets.
- **Four steps to shaping**: Set boundaries, rough out the elements, address risks and rabbit holes, and write the pitch.
  - When to use: Use the sequence for a raw idea that may become a bet.
  - How: Move from appetite and problem to solution, risk review, and pitch.

## Key Concepts
- **Level of abstraction**: The amount of detail included in a problem or solution.
- **Appetite**: The time budget that constrains the solution.
- **Rabbit hole**: An unknown or tangled problem that can multiply the original appetite.
- **Shaper**: A technically literate, strategic generalist or close collaborator who defines the concept.
- **Guard rail**: A boundary that guides work without dictating its final form.

## Mental Models
- Use **rough, solved, bounded** as a three-part test for work that is ready to pitch.
- Think of a **shaped concept** as rules for a game. The team still chooses many moves.
- Use the **two tracks** to keep exploration flexible before commitment and building focused after commitment.

## Anti-patterns
- **Wireframes are too concrete**: High-fidelity screens commit details too early, reduce design space, and hide implementation complexity.
- **Words are too abstract**: A phrase such as “build a calendar view” gives no basis for trade-offs or scope limits.
- **Unshaped hand-off**: Giving a problem without a solution pushes research and risk into a fixed cycle.

## Worked Example
Basecamp customers asked for a calendar. A full calendar could take six months, and past calendar features had only about ten percent usage. The shaped Dot Grid concept fit one six-week cycle: a two-month, read-only grid, one dot per event day, and an event list below. Tapping a dot scrolled the related events into view. The concept did not include drag-and-drop, multi-day spans, colors, or categories. The sketch stayed rough, but the behavior and boundaries were solved.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do not hand a team a polished mockup or a vague phrase.
2. Make the macro solution clear and the fine details open.
3. Require roughness, a solved approach, and a bounded appetite.
4. Keep shaping private until the team commits to a bet.
5. Use the four shaping steps in order.
