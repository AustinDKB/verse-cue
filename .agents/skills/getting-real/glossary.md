# Glossary

**80/20 Reframe** — Solve the important 80 percent of the problem with about 20 percent of the effort. (Ch 54)

**Actions, Not Words** — Judge candidates by sustained public work, not credentials or claims. (Ch 42)

**Alone Time** — A contiguous work block protected from messages, calls, meetings, and task switching. (Ch 37)

**Anti-Project** — Basecamp’s rejection of charts, reports, statistics, and command structure in project management. (Ch 8)

**API** — An access method that lets other products read or change permitted application data. (Ch 58)

**Assumption debt** — Interconnected guesses created when the builder lacks direct knowledge of the user’s problem. (Ch 5)

**Audience Language** — Customer terms that replace internal lingo, acronyms, and engineer-to-engineer wording. (Ch 52)

**Baby-Step Launch** — Release the core first, then add informed improvements through small post-launch steps. (Ch 83)

**Blank slate** — The empty first-run screen that must explain its purpose and next action. (Ch 49)

**Bloat** — Product, process, or organizational weight that does not serve a customer need. (Ch 10)

**Build Less** — Underdo competitors by solving simple problems well and leaving hairy problems outside scope. (Ch 4)

**Bug culture** — An open, blame-free way to rank, discuss, and repair defects by impact. (Ch 86)

**Code debt** — A code shortcut that creates later cleanup work and repeated repair interest. (Ch 57)

**Communication path** — A relationship through which information must travel between project members. (Ch 12)

**Context Over Consistency** — Choose the control that serves the page’s immediate task, even when it breaks repetition. (Ch 51)

**Cost of change** — The time, money, and effort needed to alter a system or product. (Ch 11)

**Customer-to-Customer Forum** — An open place where customers answer questions and share product experience. (Ch 81)

**Defensive design** — Interface work that prepares customers for failures and crisis points. (Ch 50)

**Design debt** — A good-enough or temporary design that needs later improvement. (Ch 57)

**Easy On, Easy Off** — Make signup, trial, cancellation, and data export simple and voluntary. (Ch 65)

**Emergence** — Unforeseen behavior that appears from interactions among simple parts. (Ch 11)

**Epicenter design** — Design the page’s cannot-live-without content before navigation or decoration. (Ch 47)

**Feature blight** — Feature growth that blurs the product’s outline and drains its resources. (Ch 13)

**Feature loop** — A feature that pulls in related screens, workflows, integrations, support, and promises. (Ch 24)

**Feature request** — A proposed product addition that requires judgment, not automatic acceptance. (Ch 23)

**Fight Club** — The three-day porch test that makes a feature prove it deserves implementation. (Ch 23)

**Fix Time and Budget, Flex Scope** — Keep the date and budget fixed, then remove lower-value scope. (Ch 7)

**Free Samples** — Give people a useful small offering so product value can earn further payment. (Ch 64)

**Getting Real** — Build the real interface, launch early, learn from use, and iterate with less mass. (Ch 1)

**Go With the Flow** — Follow a more plausible product direction when evidence weakens the original idea. (Ch 90)

**Grandfather period** — A stated time when existing customers keep older terms after a change. (Ch 67)

**Half, Not Half-Assed** — Cut scope until a narrow product works well instead of spreading quality across everything. (Ch 21)

**Heart-first launch** — Release the product section that carries its main value before secondary sections. (Ch 21)

**Hollywood Launch** — Use teaser, preview, and launch stages to build a permission-based audience. (Ch 68)

**Human solutions** — Give users a general framework and let them create local conventions. (Ch 26)

**Inline Upsell** — Explain a plan limit and show the upgrade that removes the blocked action. (Ch 75)

**Interface First** — Design and test the customer-facing screen before writing expensive program code. (Ch 46)

**Intelligent Inconsistency** — Vary a page deliberately when the variation makes its current task clearer. (Ch 51)

**It Just Doesn’t Matter** — Leave out detail when it does not change user behavior or essential value. (Ch 22)

**Just-in-time decisions** — Decide when a real problem and useful information make the choice necessary. (Ch 17)

**Kick the Tires** — Test a prospective hire through a small paid project before full commitment. (Ch 41)

**Less mass** — Commitments, structures, and product weight that resist a change of direction. (Ch 10)

**Less Software** — Reframe hard problems and write only the code that today’s need justifies. (Ch 54)

**Make Mantra** — A short, broad, three- or four-word statement of why an organization exists. (Ch 15)

**Name Hook** — A short, catchy, memorable product name rather than an ultradescriptive name. (Ch 76)

**One Interface** — Put regular and administrative actions in the same application context. (Ch 53)

**One-page story** — A brief plain-language account of what the application needs to do. (Ch 59)

**One-point vision** — One sentence that states a product’s purpose and difference and filters choices. (Ch 15)

**Opinionated software** — Software that takes a clear position instead of supporting every approach. (Ch 20)

**Open Doors** — Give customers feeds, APIs, widgets, and other ways to access their data. (Ch 58)

**Publicize Your Screwups** — Report failures openly, state the repair, and describe prevention steps. (Ch 82)

**Quick Story** — A short human account of a feature’s experience and flow. (Ch 61)

**Race to Running Software** — Make a working product the first priority at project and component levels. (Ch 29)

**Rinse and Repeat** — Design, use, analyze, revise, and begin another product cycle. (Ch 30)

**Scale later** — Use the simplest setup until real demand creates a measurable scale problem. (Ch 19)

**Scratching your own itch** — Build a tool for a problem the builder personally feels. (Ch 5)

**Start with no** — Let a feature request wait until repeated demand and product fit justify review. (Ch 23)

**Test in the wild** — Test a feature with real people, data, and workflows inside the real application. (Ch 34)

**The Three Musketeers** — Start version 1.0 with a developer, designer, and sweeper. (Ch 12)

**Three State Solution** — Design regular, blank, and error states for every screen. (Ch 48)

**Tough Love** — Filter customer requests and reject changes that damage the whole product. (Ch 80)

**Underdo your competition** — Choose less than competitors and solve a smaller problem well. (Ch 4)

**Wordsmiths** — People whose clear writing signals organized thought, empathy, and judgment. (Ch 45)

**Zero Training** — Make ordinary product use understandable without a manual or formal instruction. (Ch 78)
