# Chapter 64: Free Samples

## Core Idea
Give people a free, useful way to experience the product in a noisy market. A sample can earn attention, show the interface and usefulness, and lead interested people toward a paid plan.

Free does not mean empty. The offering should be small enough to try and useful enough to show the product’s value. The customer should choose more because experience earns trust, not because a contract or trick blocks exit.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Free Samples — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Give away a product, a useful version, or a free plan to let people experience the work.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the market is noisy and potential customers need evidence before they buy.
  - **How**: Offer a useful free application or plan. Let people experience the interface and value. Provide a clear path to paid plans with additional capacity or features.
- **Bite-Size Chunks — Ben McConnell and Jackie Huba, authors of Church of the Customer Blog, from “What Is Customer Evangelism?”**: Divide a product or service into a smaller offering that is inexpensive, easy, or fun to try.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the complete product feels too large or costly for a first decision.
  - **How**: Subdivide at least one offering into a simple sample. Make the first step small enough that a customer can take it without a major commitment.
- **Free Hit Single — Derek Sivers, president and programmer of CD Baby and HostBaby, from “Free Promo Track”**: Give away one strong piece of a larger collection to create desire for more.
  - **When to use**: Use it when one part of the product can demonstrate the quality of the whole.
  - **How**: Let people play, copy, share, and pass along the free piece. Trust that a strong experience can lead people to pay for more.

## Key Concepts
- **Free sample**: A no-cost offering that lets a person experience value.
- **Free plan**: A continuing product version with no payment requirement.
- **Bite-size chunk**: A smaller offering designed for an easy first step.
- **Product experience**: Direct contact with the product’s interface and usefulness.
- **Upgrade path**: A clear move from free use to a paid plan.
- **Paid additions**: More projects or pages and features such as file uploading or SSL data encryption.
- **Promotional download**: A free piece that creates interest in a larger paid collection.
- **Customer evangelism**: Customer sharing that helps other people notice and trust the product.

## Mental Models
- **Sample before promise**: Let people judge real usefulness instead of asking them to trust a sales claim.
- **Small first bite**: Lower the cost, effort, and risk of the first decision.
- **Free as a bridge**: Use no-cost access to connect attention with a paid relationship.
- **Quality earns payment**: Share enough value to create confidence that more value deserves payment.

## Anti-patterns
- **Empty free tier**: A free version without useful value cannot demonstrate why a customer should continue.
- **Free without a path**: Giving something away without a clear next plan leaves interest disconnected from revenue.
- **Oversized first step**: A sample that demands too much time or setup defeats the purpose of a bite-size offering.
- **Sharing fear**: Restricting a strong free sample prevents the attention and trust that sharing can create.
- **Forced conversion**: Making customers pay before experience replaces earned demand with pressure.

## Worked Example
Apple gave iTunes software away to build demand for the iPod and music store. Starbucks said a new purchase followed every five beverage samples. 37signals used Writeboard and Ta-da List as free applications that led people toward its other products. Its free versions let people experience the interface before choosing paid plans with more projects, pages, file uploading, or SSL data encryption. The sample created a clear reason to upgrade.

## Key Takeaways
1. Give potential customers a useful way to try the product.
2. Make at least one offering small, easy, or fun to use.
3. Let the interface and usefulness demonstrate value directly.
4. State what paid plans add, such as capacity or features.
5. Share a strong free piece with confidence that quality creates demand.
6. Keep the first step small without making it empty.
