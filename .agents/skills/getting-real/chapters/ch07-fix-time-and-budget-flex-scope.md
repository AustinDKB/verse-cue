# Chapter 7: Fix Time and Budget, Flex Scope

## Core Idea
Fix the launch date and the budget, then reduce scope until the work fits. A smaller, quality release is better than a full-scope release with holes and weak quality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fix time and budget, flex scope — Basecamp**: Use this rule when a release has a fixed date or a fixed amount of money. Do not add time or money to rescue every feature. Remove work instead.
  - **When to use**: Use it during release planning and whenever the requested work exceeds available resources.
  - **How**: Keep the date and budget fixed. Identify the initial release work, rank its importance, and remove lower-value scope. Add the removed work later when the product and business can support it.
- **Prioritization, reality, and flexibility — Basecamp**: Use fixed time and budget to force hard decisions, set honest expectations, and keep room for changes based on real building experience.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team keeps delaying a decision or tries to promise time, money, and scope at the same time.
  - **How**: Decide what matters most, protect quality, and keep scope as the adjustable part of the plan.
- **One day at a time — Fred Brooks**: Use Brooks’s schedule rule when a project falls behind through small additions. A year of delay can grow from one extra day at a time.
  - **When to use**: Use it when small requests appear harmless but repeatedly extend the schedule.
  - **How**: Find the added work that consumes each day. Remove or defer it before the delay becomes a new normal.

## Key Concepts
- **Fixed time**: A launch date that does not expand to fit more work.
- **Fixed budget**: A spending limit that does not expand to fit more work.
- **Flexible scope**: The portion of the plan that can shrink or change.
- **Initial release**: The smaller product that ships first.
- **Prioritization**: The choice of what enters the initial release.
- **Quality**: The standard protected by reducing scope.
- **Reality**: The limit created by actual time, money, and work.
- **Scope creep**: Added work that turns small daily changes into delay.

## Mental Models
- **Scope as the release valve**: When time and budget stay fixed, use scope to absorb new information.
- **The quality triangle**: Trying to fix time, budget, and scope together usually lowers quality.
- **Now versus later**: Later is eternal, but now is fleeting. Ship the essential part now and keep additions for later.
- **One day at a time**: Small additions can create a large schedule problem when nobody removes them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fixing time, budget, and scope together**: This creates a promise that rarely holds and often sacrifices quality.
- **Throwing time or money at every problem**: It protects scope at the cost of the business and hides poor prioritization.
- **Waiting for a magical window**: A perfect date, budget, and full scope combination is not a sound release plan.
- **Hemming and hawing**: Delayed choices let low-value work consume the resources needed for important work.
- **Launching a mediocre full product**: A product full of holes damages the release more than a smaller focused product does.

## Worked Example
Assume the team has a fixed launch date and budget, but the planned features do not fit. Keep both limits. Select the work that makes the first release useful, remove the rest, and launch the smaller product at a high quality level. Record the removed features for later releases. This decision follows Basecamp’s rule that a smaller great product is better than a full product that misses its quality target.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fix the date and budget before selecting the release scope.
2. Remove lower-value work when the plan does not fit.
3. Use scope changes to protect quality.
4. Make hard priority decisions early.
5. Treat small daily additions as schedule risks.
6. Add deferred work later instead of delaying the first release.
