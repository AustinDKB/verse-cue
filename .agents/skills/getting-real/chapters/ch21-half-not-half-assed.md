# Chapter 21: Half, Not Half-Assed

## Core Idea
Build half a product that works well instead of a whole product with weak versions of every idea. Cut the scope until only the essential work remains, then let real use guide the next addition.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Half, not half-assed — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use repeated subtraction to protect quality and focus.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team collects every decent idea or treats a broad feature list as a complete product plan.
  - **How**: Take the proposed product and cut it in half. Remove more until the essential work remains. Table good ideas that do not support the first useful version, then add only after the foundation gains traction.
- **Heart-first launch — Basecamp team**: Start with the part that carries the product's main value.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the product contains several sections but one section clearly represents its purpose.
  - **How**: Build and release the central section first. Observe real-world usage before committing to milestones, to-do lists, or other secondary sections.

## Key Concepts
- **Half product**: A deliberately narrow product that does its essential work well.
- **Half-assed product**: A broad product with weak, unfinished versions of many parts.
- **Essential feature**: A feature required for the first useful product.
- **Scope cut**: Removal of work until the product has a manageable shape.
- **Kitchen sink**: The attempt to include every decent idea in one release.
- **Traction**: Evidence that a product gains use and interest.
- **Real-world usage**: Behavior that replaces guesses about what users need.
- **Foundation**: The solid first version that supports later additions.

## Mental Models
- **Wedge before platform**: Start with the narrow part that proves the product's value.
- **Quality concentration**: Put limited time into fewer features so those features work well.
- **Subtraction twice**: Cut the proposed scope in half, then test whether it needs another cut.
- **Evidence over hunches**: Let use decide what the foundation should gain next.

## Anti-patterns
- **Everything but the kitchen sink**: Too many features spread attention and produce a weak product.
- **Adding every decent idea**: A good idea can still be wrong for the first release.
- **Building from hunches**: Secondary work may reflect assumptions that real users would reject.
- **Expanding before traction**: New sections add cost before the product proves its foundation.
- **Confusing breadth with completeness**: More sections do not make an unclear product useful.

## Worked Example
Basecamp began with only its messages section because the team considered messages the heart of the application. It ignored milestones, to-do lists, and other sections for the time being. That narrow release let the team observe real-world usage instead of guessing what the full product needed. Later additions could rest on a foundation that users had already tested.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cut the proposed product in half before building it.
2. Cut it again when the essential work remains unclear.
3. Start with the section that carries the product's main value.
4. Table good ideas until real usage proves their need.
5. Add to a solid foundation only after the product gains traction.
