# Chapter 23: Start With No

## Core Idea
Make features work hard to earn implementation. Each yes creates a long chain of design, implementation, and testing, then creates a lasting support obligation. Start with no, listen, and reconsider only when a request proves that it survives.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Start with no — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use no as the default response to a new feature request.
  - **When to use**: Use it for every request from a customer or from the team, especially when the request does not support the product's central vision.
  - **How**: Listen without acting. Say “not now.” Watch whether the request returns. Revisit it only after repeated demand gives a reason for deeper review.
- **Make each feature work hard to be implemented — Basecamp team**: Require evidence before a feature enters the product.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a feature sounds attractive but has no proof of repeated need.
  - **How**: Let the request stand outside the product. Use the “Fight Club” test and let it wait on the porch for three days. Treat it as a survivor only when it keeps returning and fits the product's purpose.
- **Say NO by default — Steve Jobs, as recalled by Derek Sivers**: Protect innovation by rejecting all but crucial features.
  - **When to use**: Use it when people present a long list of possible additions.
  - **How**: Do not measure innovation by the number of accepted ideas. Choose only the crucial features that keep the product clear and useful.

## Key Concepts
- **Default no**: The first response that prevents immediate scope growth.
- **Not now**: A decision to listen without committing to implementation.
- **Feature request**: A proposed product addition from a customer or team member.
- **Survivor**: A request that returns often enough to deserve deeper review.
- **Implementation chain**: The design, implementation, testing, and support work created by a feature.
- **Feature permanence**: The difficulty of removing a released feature after customers depend on it.
- **Crucial feature**: A feature whose value justifies its lasting cost.
- **Yes-man**: A person or team that accepts requests without judgment.

## Mental Models
- **Feature as adoption**: Treat every yes like adopting a child that needs care for its entire life.
- **Porch test**: Let a proposed feature wait outside until it proves that it belongs.
- **Repeated demand as evidence**: A request that returns has stronger evidence than a single suggestion.
- **No protects identity**: A small feature set gives customers a reason to like the product's focus.

## Anti-patterns
- **Immediate yes**: The team accepts a request before it knows the design, test, and support cost.
- **Yes-man behavior**: Automatic agreement turns the product into a list of other people's wishes.
- **Feature adoption without exit**: Released features become hard to remove and continue to consume resources.
- **Pleasing everyone**: The product loses the focused behavior that attracted its best customers.
- **A thousand features**: A large feature count makes the product ugly, unclear, and harder to use.

## Worked Example
During a private presentation of the iTunes Music Store, people repeatedly asked whether the service had, or would add, another feature. Steve Jobs stopped the questions and explained the decision. The team knew that people could suggest a thousand features, but it did not want a thousand features. Innovation meant saying no to all but the most crucial features. Derek Sivers used this example in “Say NO by default” to show why rejection protects a clear product.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start every feature request with no or “not now.”
2. Listen for repeated demand before doing deeper analysis.
3. Count the full implementation and support chain before saying yes.
4. Keep only features that fit the product's vision and prove their value.
5. Use no to protect the focus customers already value.
