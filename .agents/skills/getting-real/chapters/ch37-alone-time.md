# Chapter 37: Alone Time

## Core Idea
People need a long, uninterrupted block to reach the zone where real progress happens. Protect a contiguous part of each workday from messages, calls, meetings, and other interruptions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Alone time — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Reserve part of the day for uninterrupted work.
  - **When to use**: Use it when communication fragments the day or when people struggle to finish demanding work.
  - **How**: Make half the day alone time, such as 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., or choose the first or last half. Keep the block contiguous and permit only lunch or another stated exception.
- **The zone — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Protect the concentrated state in which a person avoids task switching and makes real progress.
  - **When to use**: Use it for development, design, writing, or any work that needs sustained thought.
  - **How**: Stop instant messages, phone calls, meetings, and email threads that need an immediate response. Let people work without answering every interruption.
- **Flow and interruption cost — Joel Spolsky**: Treat interruption as a restart of concentration, not as a one-minute event.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team underestimates the cost of a quick question or message.
  - **How**: Measure the recovery time after an interruption. Reduce noise because a one-minute interruption can take about half an hour to restore productive concentration.

## Key Concepts
- **Alone time**: A protected work period without normal communication.
- **Zone**: A state of deep concentration and high productivity.
- **Flow**: Full concentration on the task with little awareness of surroundings.
- **Contiguous block**: One uninterrupted period rather than several short periods.
- **Task switching**: Moving attention between different tasks.
- **Interruption**: An event that removes attention from the current task.
- **Communication addiction**: A repeated need to check and answer messages.
- **Recovery time**: The time needed to regain productive concentration.
- **Overlap**: The part of the day when distributed workers work together.

## Mental Models
- **Zone entry cost**: Concentration needs time before it produces its best work, like REM sleep after sleep begins.
- **Interruption as restart**: A small interruption can erase more than its clock time.
- **Contiguous beats scattered**: One long block protects concentration better than several short pauses.
- **Distributed-work advantage**: Different time zones can create useful alone time outside shared hours.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fragmented alone time**: Several short quiet periods do not give the mind enough time to reach the zone.
- **Communication addiction**: Constant message checking prevents sustained work.
- **Meeting-filled workday**: Meetings divide the day into pieces that do not support concentration.
- **Quick-question blindness**: A team counts only the interruption minute and ignores recovery time.
- **False availability**: People remain reachable for every message and lose the work period they need.

## Worked Example
Basecamp’s team spread across four cities and eight time zones. Only about four or five hours overlapped, while the remaining hours gave people time alone. The team found that the alone part produced the most work because people did not need to switch tasks for questions, email, or instant messages. A local team can copy this pattern by protecting 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., except during lunch. During that block, people stop messages, calls, meetings, and urgent-response email. The block stays whole so workers can reach the zone and remain there.

## Key Takeaways
1. Protect one contiguous half-day for focused work.
2. Stop instant messages, calls, and meetings during that period.
3. Count recovery time when you judge an interruption.
4. Use distributed schedules to create additional alone time.
5. Let people work without constant communication access.
