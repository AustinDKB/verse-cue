# Chapter 63: Personify Your Product

## Core Idea
Treat the product as a person with a clear personality. Choose the traits that should guide the product’s voice, interface, copywriting, and feature set.

The product speaks to customers all day. A personality gives the team a consistent test for change. Before accepting a new feature, message, or interaction, ask whether it fits the person the product wants to be.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Personify Your Product — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Define the product as if it were a person with specific traits.
  - **When to use**: Use it before the team makes broad copy, interface, or feature choices.
  - **How**: Choose traits such as polite, stern, forgiving, strict, funny, serious, trusting, modest, or likable. Use the selected traits to guide each product decision.
- **Personality Fit Test — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Test every change against the product’s chosen character.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a new feature, message, or visual change seems useful but changes the product’s tone.
  - **How**: Ask whether the change sounds, behaves, and helps like the product’s chosen person. Keep it when the answer fits and change it when it does not.

## Key Concepts
- **Product personality**: The selected human traits that shape product decisions.
- **Product voice**: The tone that customers hear through words and behavior.
- **Trait**: A chosen quality such as modest, strict, funny, or trusting.
- **Copywriting**: The words that communicate the product’s attitude and guidance.
- **Interface behavior**: The way controls, messages, and flows act toward the customer.
- **Feature set**: The collection of abilities that the product offers.
- **Personality fit**: Agreement between a change and the product’s intended character.

## Mental Models
- **Product as a person**: Think of every screen as an interaction with the same character.
- **Voice consistency**: Use one chosen personality to keep copy and behavior coherent.
- **Character filter**: Reject a change that solves a local need but makes the whole product act like someone else.

## Anti-patterns
- **Personality drift**: Adding unrelated tones makes the product feel inconsistent and difficult to understand.
- **Feature without character**: A useful feature can still harm the product when its behavior conflicts with the chosen traits.
- **Voice by accident**: Unchosen words and interactions make the product speak through default team habits.
- **Trait switching**: Changing from trusting to paranoid, or from modest to know-it-all, without a reason breaks customer expectations.

## Worked Example
A team chooses a product that is modest, likable, and forgiving. It writes copy that helps without boasting, designs an interface that lets people correct mistakes, and selects features that support that behavior. When a proposed change adds a stern warning and a rigid flow, the team applies the personality fit test. It changes the warning and flow so the product remains helpful and forgiving instead of accepting a local feature that changes its character.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose the product’s personality before making broad decisions.
2. Record a small set of clear human traits.
3. Use the traits to guide copy, interface behavior, and features.
4. Test every change against the same product character.
5. Treat the product’s voice as a continuous customer interaction.
