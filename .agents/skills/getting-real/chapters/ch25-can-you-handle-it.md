# Chapter 25: Can You Handle It?

## Core Idea
Build products and offer services that the team can manage. A promise has value only when the organization can sustain it with its systems, strategy, staff, and finances.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Build something you can manage — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Match each product promise to the capacity that supports it.
  - **When to use**: Use it before launching a program, free allowance, service level, or other commitment that creates recurring work.
  - **How**: Check the organizational, strategic, and financial burden. Identify the systems, records, staff actions, and payments needed to deliver the promise over time. Reduce the promise when the full burden does not fit.
- **Credit instead of cash payout — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Choose a manageable reward mechanism when a program needs accounting and payouts.
  - **When to use**: Use it when an affiliate program would require repeated checks, signatures, mailing, and payment administration.
  - **How**: Consider credit against membership fees as a simpler alternative. Select the option that the existing operation can track and sustain.
- **Start small — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Limit an offer until the team understands its true cost.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a free allowance or service promise exceeds known capacity.
  - **How**: Offer less, reserve the offer for paying accounts, or delay it. Expand only after the operation proves that it can support the larger promise.

## Key Concepts
- **Manageable product**: A product whose promises fit available operational capacity.
- **Sustainability**: The ability to keep delivering a promise over time.
- **Organizational cost**: Staff, process, and system work needed to operate an offer.
- **Strategic cost**: The effect of a promise on product direction and attention.
- **Financial cost**: Money required to deliver, track, and pay for an offer.
- **Affiliate program**: A reward system that creates accounting and payout work.
- **Membership credit**: A reward applied against a customer membership fee.
- **Free allowance**: A no-cost resource limit promised to a user.

## Mental Models
- **Promise as a recurring bill**: Treat every offer as work that arrives again after launch.
- **Capacity before comparison**: Do not copy a larger competitor until your own operation can support the same promise.
- **Small first promise**: Start with a limit that gives the team room to learn and respond.
- **Manageability triangle**: Test organizational, strategic, and financial fit together.

## Anti-patterns
- **Promise first, system later**: Announce an offer before building the records and process that deliver it.
- **Competitor imitation**: Match a large company without matching its staff, systems, or budget.
- **Unlimited free capacity**: Give away a large resource allowance without knowing its continuing cost.
- **Cash-payout burden**: Choose checks and mailing when a simpler credit system meets the same goal.
- **Unsustainable generosity**: Make a promise that creates more obligation than value.

## Worked Example
An affiliate program can sound attractive, but it requires accounting for each referral and repeated payouts. The team might need to write, sign, and mail a check every month. A membership-credit reward can meet the same purpose with less payment administration. The same test applies to storage. Instead of copying a competitor's free one-gigabyte offer, the team can start at one hundred megabytes or reserve storage for paying accounts. Each choice reduces the promise until the team can deliver it with confidence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test organizational, strategic, and financial capacity before launch.
2. Treat every promise as a continuing obligation.
3. Prefer a simple reward process when it meets the same goal.
4. Start with a small allowance when the full cost remains uncertain.
5. Expand only after the operation proves that it can sustain the offer.
