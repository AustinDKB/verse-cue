# Chapter 13: Embrace Constraints

## Core Idea
Use limits on time, money, and people as guides for focus and innovation. Do not spend all your effort removing constraints. Work with what you have, keep the plate small, and solve one part at a time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Embrace constraints — Basecamp**: Use this framework when a project has too little time, money, or manpower.
  - **When to use**: Use it at the start of planning and whenever the team feels the “not enough” blues.
  - **How**: List the limits, keep the plate small, break large tasks into small pieces, tackle one piece at a time, and prioritize as you move. Let the limits decide what the team can carry.
- **Work with what you have — Basecamp**: Use existing conditions as operating advantages instead of waiting for venture capital, long release cycles, or quick hires.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team wants outside resources before testing a workable idea.
  - **How**: Build less software to lower the cost of change. Give people enough features to solve their problems in their own way, and communicate with the tools and time already available.
- **Fight feature blight — Jef Raskin**: Use a constricting deadline to resist “creeping elegance,” which Raskin describes as feature blight.
  - **When to use**: Use it when features gradually blur the product’s true outline and drain its resources.
  - **How**: Discard features according to the time needed to implement them, but remember the warning that the most useful features often take the longest. A deadline can remove features without automatically producing a useful product.

## Key Concepts
- **Constraint**: A limit that guides focus and choice.
- **Innovation**: A creative solution produced under limits.
- **Focus**: Attention placed on the work that matters most.
- **Small plate**: A deliberately limited amount of work in progress.
- **Task breakdown**: Splitting a large task into small pieces.
- **Cost of change**: The effort needed to alter software after it grows.
- **Feature blight**: Feature growth that obscures the product and drains its resources.
- **Constricting deadline**: A deadline that forces feature removal.
- **Communication efficiency**: Faster, clearer communication created by distance and limited contact.

## Mental Models
- **Limit as a guide**: Treat “not enough” as information about what belongs in the product.
- **Small plate**: Put only a manageable amount of work in front of the team.
- **One piece at a time**: Break the problem until the next action fits the available capacity.
- **Constraint in disguise**: A limitation can create a better process, such as short direct messages across a time difference.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trying to remove every limit**: More resources can expand the plan instead of improving focus.
- **Scope overload**: A large plate prevents the team from finishing the most important work.
- **Feature blight**: Gradual feature growth blurs the product’s outline and drains its resources.
- **Waiting for venture capital or quick hires**: Delayed action hides whether the idea works.
- **Long, unfocused communication**: Extra ceremony can waste the scarce time that constraints should sharpen.

## Worked Example
Basecamp had a design firm to run, existing client work, a seven-hour time difference, a small team, and no outside funding. The team kept its plate small, broke large tasks into pieces, moved step by step, and prioritized as it went. It built less software, gave people enough features to solve problems their own way, and used instant messages and email almost exclusively. The distance forced direct communication and became an efficiency advantage.

## Key Takeaways
1. List the limits before you plan the work.
2. Keep the work in progress small.
3. Break large tasks into pieces that fit current capacity.
4. Build less software and give users enough control to solve problems.
5. Use distance and limited contact to make communication direct.
6. Watch for feature blight when the product grows.
7. Use a deadline to remove scope, but judge usefulness separately.
