# Chapter 15: What's the Big Idea?

## Core Idea
Define the product's one-point vision before design or code. A brief vision explains why the product exists, separates it from similar products, and guides later decisions toward one consistent result.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **One-point vision — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman, Basecamp**: Use this when a product needs a clear purpose and a test for difficult choices.
  - **When to use**: Use it before design and code, then return to it whenever a decision reaches a sticking point.
  - **How**: Write one sentence that states the product's purpose and difference. Ask, “Are we staying true to the vision?” Remove work that does not support the answer.
- **Whiteboard philosophy — Dave Thomas and Andy Hunt**: Use visible maxims to guide difficult decisions in complex work.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team must choose between competing failure modes or priorities.
  - **How**: Write a short rule where the team can see it. Dave Thomas and Andy Hunt used “Err in favor of users” for a debit-card transaction switch. Keep several such laws visible and apply them together.
- **Make Mantra — Guy Kawasaki**: Use a short statement as an organizational guidepost.
  - **When to use**: Use it when people need a simple answer to why the organization exists and what motivates the work.
  - **How**: Describe the purpose in three or four words. Make the phrase short, broad, and easy to remember.

## Key Concepts
- **Vision**: A brief statement of the product's purpose and difference.
- **One-point vision**: A single sentence that keeps product decisions consistent.
- **Guidepost**: A short rule that helps people choose during uncertain work.
- **Maxim**: A concise law that directs repeated decisions.
- **Collective ownership**: Shared responsibility created when participants understand and join the work.
- **Transparency**: Open access to communication and project information.
- **Communication priority**: A feature choice that supports messages, comments, tasks, or shared files.
- **Mantra**: A three- or four-word description of why an organization exists.

## Mental Models
- **Vision as a filter**: Treat every proposed feature as a test of the product's purpose.
- **Communication as the center**: For Basecamp, treat project management as communication, not as reports and charts.
- **Visible law**: Put a short decision rule in view so the team can use it during complex work.
- **Small sentence, large reach**: Use a short purpose statement to guide many later choices.

## Anti-patterns
- **Starting with design or code**: Without a purpose, local choices pull the product in different directions.
- **Vague vision**: A broad or decorative statement cannot resolve a difficult decision.
- **Optimizing for reports first**: Charts, graphs, tables, statistics, and spreadsheets can displace the communication the product needs.
- **Hidden participation**: Restricting access to project communication blocks ownership and involvement from clients or other participants.
- **Unwritten principles**: Teams repeat debate when their decision rules remain only in memory.

## Worked Example
Basecamp used the vision “Project management is communication.” The team believed effective communication created ownership, involvement, investment, momentum, and a shared goal. The vision led Basecamp to stay open and transparent, give clients access, and favor participation over elaborate permissions. It also led the team to skip charts, graphs, tables, reports, statistics, and spreadsheets. Messages, comments, to-do lists, and file sharing received priority instead.

The other product visions made the same choice visible: “Bring life’s loose ends together” for Backpack, “Group chat over IM sucks” for Campfire, “Competing with a post-it note” for Ta-da List, and “Word is overkill” for Writeboard. Each sentence gives later decisions a direction.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write the product vision in one sentence before building.
2. Use the vision to settle hard choices and remove unrelated work.
3. Put important decision rules where the team can see them.
4. Prefer the product's central purpose over familiar but unnecessary features.
5. Give the organization a short mantra that people can remember.
