# Chapter 61: Tell Me a Quick Story

## Core Idea
Write a brief human story when words must explain a feature or concept. Describe the experience and its flow, not technical or visual detail.

The story starts conversation and points the team in a useful direction. The team can settle tactics while it builds the relevant part of the application. If the story appears beside the screens under development, people can connect the intended experience to a visible product.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Quick Story — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Explain a feature as a short account of what a person does and experiences.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a new feature needs explanation before the team has enough information for detailed design.
  - **How**: Describe the human flow in normal conversation. Keep the account brief and omit technical and visual details.
- **Experience Before Tactics — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Start with the customer experience instead of selecting implementation details too soon.
  - **When to use**: Use it at the start of a feature discussion or when a team argues about tactics before it understands the goal.
  - **How**: State what happens from the person’s point of view. Let building determine the technical and design choices later.
- **Story Beside the Screen — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Put the short story in context with the screens that the team develops.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a flow needs shared understanding across design, development, and product work.
  - **How**: Pair the human account with the current screens. Use both artifacts to start a conversation and find the next step.

## Key Concepts
- **Quick story**: A short description of a human feature flow.
- **Human flow**: The sequence of actions and results that a person experiences.
- **Experience**: What the person does, sees, and gets from the feature.
- **Strategy**: The direction and purpose that guide a feature.
- **Tactics**: Detailed choices that emerge during building.
- **Conversation starter**: A small artifact that helps people discuss the right problem.
- **Screen context**: The visible interface placed beside the story.

## Mental Models
- **Story before blueprint**: Use a human flow to begin work when detailed requirements would force guesses.
- **Strategy, then tactics**: Decide what the person needs before deciding how the system will do it.
- **Conversation over completion**: Treat the story as a starting point, not a finished specification.

## Anti-patterns
- **Detail dump**: Technical and design details hide the experience that the team must understand first.
- **Essay feature**: A long document slows discussion and invites premature decisions.
- **Tactic-first planning**: Choosing implementation details before the goal causes the team to solve the wrong problem.
- **Context-free story**: A story kept away from developing screens loses the chance to connect words with experience.

## Worked Example
A team needs to explain a new feature. It writes a brief account of the person’s flow in ordinary language, without naming database choices, screen details, or implementation steps. It places that account beside the screens under development. The team now has enough context to discuss the experience and choose its next action. As the team builds, tactics fall into place and the story can change when the experience becomes clearer.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write a short human story instead of a detailed feature document.
2. Describe the experience before selecting tactics.
3. Use ordinary language that starts a useful conversation.
4. Place the story beside the screens under development.
5. Let building reveal the technical and design details.
