# Chapter 85: Better, Not Beta

## Core Idea
Do not use “beta” as a permanent excuse. An endless public beta tells customers that the company has not committed to a finished product and shifts responsibility for defects onto the people who use it.

Private betas are useful for controlled testing. A public product should be good enough for public use. Do not wait for perfection, because perfection will not arrive. Take responsibility for the release, name it as a release, and improve it without hiding behind a label.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Better, Not Beta — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Release a product as a real product when it meets the public quality bar.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team wants to expose a product publicly but feels tempted to call it beta to avoid commitment.
  - **How**: Test privately when needed. Decide whether the product is good enough for public consumption. If it is, release it and accept responsibility for what customers receive.
- **Private Beta versus Public Release — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Separate controlled testing from public availability.
  - **When to use**: Use a private beta when the team needs a limited group to find problems before broad release.
  - **How**: Keep the beta private during testing. Do not give the public an unfinished product and use the beta label to excuse weak quality.
- **Beta Is Meaningless — Mary Hodder, information architect and interaction designer, from The Definition of Beta**: Recognize that repeated public beta use can make the label lose its meaning.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team assumes that the word beta will explain or excuse every defect.
  - **How**: Judge the product itself. Google and other developers have trained users to see beta as meaningless. Do not rely on a status word to communicate commitment or quality.
- **All the Time — Jim Coudal, founder of Coudal Partners**: Notice the danger of treating every product as permanently unfinished.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team delays a clear release by treating normal improvement as a permanent pre-release state.
  - **How**: Accept that products continue to improve after release. Mark the point at which the team owns the public product.

## Key Concepts
- **Beta**: A pre-release label that can become an excuse when it lasts forever.
- **Private beta**: Limited testing with a selected group before public release.
- **Public release**: A product offered for public use with company responsibility.
- **Public consumption**: Use by the general customer population.
- **Commitment**: The company’s acceptance of responsibility for the product.
- **Quality bar**: The standard that decides whether a product is ready for public use.
- **Perfection**: An unreachable condition that should not block a useful release.
- **Release responsibility**: Ownership of the product and its defects after public launch.

## Mental Models
- **A label cannot replace quality**: Calling a product beta does not make an unfinished experience acceptable.
- **Release is a promise**: Public availability tells customers that the company owns what it offers.
- **Private testing, public accountability**: Test with a limited group, then release with clear responsibility.

## Anti-patterns
- **Interminable beta**: A product stays in beta forever and tells customers that commitment will come later.
- **Beta scapegoat**: The company uses the label to say that defects are not its responsibility.
- **Perfection delay**: The team waits for an impossible final state instead of releasing a useful product.
- **Public unfinished test**: The team exposes the broad public to a product that is not good enough for public consumption.

## Worked Example
A team has a useful core product but still sees improvements it wants to make. It tests the product with a limited private group first. When the product meets the public quality bar, the team calls it a release, not a public beta. It accepts responsibility for defects, fixes them after release, and does not use normal future improvement as proof that the product was never ready.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use private beta testing when controlled feedback is useful.
2. Do not expose the public to an unfinished product under a beta label.
3. Release when the product is good enough, not when perfection arrives.
4. Treat public release as acceptance of responsibility.
5. Let the product improve without keeping it permanently pre-release.
