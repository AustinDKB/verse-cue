# Chapter 44: You Can't Fake Enthusiasm

## Core Idea
Choose a happy and enthusiastic person over a frustrated expert when the expert lacks trust, energy, or fit. Enthusiasm shows whether a candidate wants to build the product, work alone when needed, and join the team’s direction.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Happy and average over frustrated and great — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Prefer a steady, enthusiastic employee over a disgruntled expert.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a famous expert and a less famous candidate both meet the basic skill need.
  - **How**: Look for trust, self-direction, excitement about the product, shared dislike of the problems, and a clear wish to join the team.
- **Extra points for asking questions — Eric Stephens**: Use a candidate’s questions as evidence of passion and understanding.
  - **When to use**: Use it during an interview or working discussion about the project.
  - **How**: Notice whether the candidate asks clarifying questions, explores different implementations, and seeks exact details before proposing solutions.

## Key Concepts
- **Enthusiasm**: Real interest in the work that a candidate wants to do.
- **Happy employee**: A person who brings energy and trust to daily work.
- **Frustrated expert**: A highly skilled person whose dissatisfaction harms the team.
- **Tech celebrity**: A famous expert who may bring status but not useful team behavior.
- **Self-direction**: The ability to get work done when left alone.
- **Shared problem**: A product challenge or dislike that the candidate understands and accepts.
- **Clarifying question**: A question that makes the problem and desired behavior more exact.

## Mental Models
- **Energy is evidence**: Sustained excitement about the work matters more than status.
- **Question signal**: Good questions show that a candidate wants to understand the problem before acting.
- **Fit before fame**: Evaluate daily trust and shared direction before a public reputation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Celebrity bias**: A famous name can hide poor teamwork or a primadonna attitude.
- **Expertise-only hiring**: Skill without happiness can create friction and slow the team.
- **Passive interview**: A candidate who asks nothing may not care enough to understand the work.
- **Fake enthusiasm test**: A short burst of excitement cannot replace questions, trust, and evidence of follow-through.

## Worked Example
During a project discussion, a passionate programmer asks how the web app should work, tests several possible interpretations, and proposes improvements. The team follows the questions into detail. That exchange reveals whether the candidate understands that many implementations exist and whether the candidate fits the team’s way of solving problems.

## Key Takeaways
1. Prefer enthusiasm over fame when both candidates meet the skill need.
2. Check whether the person can work without constant direction.
3. Look for excitement about the product and its problems.
4. Give extra weight to useful clarifying questions.
5. Judge cultural fit while discussing real project details.
