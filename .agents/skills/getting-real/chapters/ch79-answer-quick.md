# Chapter 79: Answer Quick

## Core Idea
Make quick, thoughtful support a top priority. Customers expect canned replies that arrive days later, so a direct answer within minutes or hours can distinguish a small team from a larger competitor.

A perfect answer is not required first. A quick, honest acknowledgement buys goodwill, shows that the team listens, and can move an angry customer toward a polite conversation. Customers also form an army of many. They report bugs, reveal needs, spread the message, and build community.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Answer Quick — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Give support requests a fast, thoughtful response.
  - **When to use**: Use it for every customer question or complaint, especially during business hours.
  - **How**: Answer directly and honestly. Basecamp reports answering 90 percent of email support requests within 90 minutes, often within a half-hour. Do not wait for a perfect final answer before acknowledging the customer.
- **Goodwill Through Honest Acknowledgement — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use an open interim answer when the requested repair cannot happen at once.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a customer reports a problem that the team cannot fix immediately.
  - **How**: Confirm that the team heard the concern. State that the issue needs future work. Do not promise an immediate repair that the team cannot deliver.
- **An Army of Many — Michael Reining, co-founder of MindValley and Blinklist**: Treat customers as a community that helps a small team compete with larger companies.
  - **When to use**: Use it from the first customer onward, while the product still has bugs and unmet needs.
  - **How**: Treat customers as the most important asset and like royalty. Thank them for bug reports. Listen for needs. Let customers carry the message. Give developers direct access to feedback and online discussion.
- **Release Early and Often — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Release before perfection and use customer contact to improve the product.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a product has a useful core but still lacks polish or every requested feature.
  - **How**: Release the core product. Thank customers who report bugs. Acknowledge that the product listens and cares instead of pretending that every issue has a solution now.

## Key Concepts
- **Turnaround time**: The time between a customer request and the team’s response.
- **Canned response**: A standard reply that can feel delayed and impersonal.
- **Goodwill**: Customer trust gained through quick and honest care.
- **Directness**: Clear communication without hiding behind vague language.
- **Army of many**: Customers who report problems, reveal needs, and spread the product message.
- **Customer community**: Users who help the team and help one another.
- **Acknowledgement**: A clear sign that the team heard and recorded a concern.
- **Release early and often**: A practice of shipping useful product increments before perfection.

## Mental Models
- **Speed signals care**: A fast answer shows that a person, not only a queue, received the message.
- **A response can precede a repair**: Acknowledging the problem preserves trust while the team decides what to do.
- **Customers extend a small team**: Many users can find bugs, explain needs, and carry the product message.

## Anti-patterns
- **Delayed canned reply**: A late standard message confirms the customer’s expectation that nobody listens.
- **Silence until certainty**: Waiting for a final solution makes a real problem feel ignored.
- **Perfect-launch dependency**: Refusing to release until every feature and bug is complete delays useful learning.
- **Detached development**: Keeping developers away from feedback prevents the team from hearing the customer voice.

## Worked Example
Blinklist answered every customer email, usually within the first hour, unless the team was asleep. Its online forum received an acknowledgement for every post and comment. Developers received the feedback and joined the discussions. This created a community that reported bugs, described needs, and helped carry the product message. Basecamp used the same speed principle by answering most business-hour email requests within 90 minutes.

## Key Takeaways
1. Answer customer messages quickly, even when the final repair needs more time.
2. Use direct acknowledgement to buy goodwill.
3. Thank customers who report bugs or unmet needs.
4. Let developers read and join customer discussions.
5. Release useful product before perfection and listen after release.
6. Build a community that can help the product compete with larger companies.
