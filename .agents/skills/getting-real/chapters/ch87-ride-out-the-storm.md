# Chapter 87: Ride Out the Storm

## Core Idea
A product change can create immediate noise. Adding a feature, changing a policy, or removing something can rock the boat. The first reactions often arrive before people use the change. Wait through the initial 24–48 hours, let passions settle, then judge the larger response. Loud negative voices do not prove that most customers reject a necessary decision.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ride Out the Storm — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Wait until knee-jerk reactions to a change die down before taking action.
  - **When to use**: Use it after a new feature, policy change, or removal creates strong customer reactions.
  - **How**: Resist panic. Take in the early responses. Wait 24–48 hours. Then make a reasoned response after people have used the change or lived with its removal.
- **The 24–48-Hour Reaction Window — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Treat the first one or two days as an unstable period.
  - **When to use**: Use it when early comments demand a rapid rollback or another rapid change.
  - **How**: Do not move during the first flare of passion. Let initial emotion lose force before deciding.
- **Negative Voices Are Louder — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Separate response volume from the response of the whole customer base.
  - **When to use**: Use it when negative comments dominate the messages you receive.
  - **How**: Remember that negative reactions often sound louder and more passionate than positive reactions. Do not treat the loudest voices as the majority. Keep a necessary but controversial decision when the broader evidence supports it.

## Key Concepts
- **Knee-jerk reaction**: An immediate response made before a person has used or understood a change.
- **Rock the boat**: Change an existing product or policy in a way that creates customer waves.
- **Initial reaction period**: The first 24–48 hours after a change, when passions can flare.
- **Reasoned response**: A decision made after early emotion settles and more evidence arrives.
- **Negative voice**: A customer response that sounds urgent because dissatisfaction encourages strong expression.
- **Positive response**: Approval that may remain quiet even when most customers accept a change.
- **Backpedal**: Reverse a decision too quickly because of early pressure.
- **Controversial decision**: A necessary choice that receives strong disagreement from some customers.

## Mental Models
- **Weather before navigation**: Treat the first wave as weather, not as the full direction of the sea.
- **Use time as a filter**: Let time remove the emotion that hides useful evidence.
- **Volume is not reach**: Count who supports or rejects a change instead of counting only message intensity.
- **Change needs a use period**: Judge a feature after customers use it, not only after they first see it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Panic rollback**: Reversing a change immediately lets the first emotional wave control the product.
- **Instant reaction management**: Rapidly changing the product after every early comment prevents a stable judgment.
- **Loudness-as-majority**: Treating negative volume as proof that most customers are unhappy misreads the customer base.
- **Foolish backpedaling**: Abandoning a necessary decision because it is controversial sacrifices the product’s direction.
- **Premature verdict**: Judging a change before people have used it confuses first contact with real experience.

## Worked Example
A team introduces a feature and receives angry comments during the first day. It does not roll back the feature at once. The team waits 24–48 hours, observes how customers use it, and checks whether the negative voices represent most customers. If the feature remains necessary and the broader response supports it, the team keeps the decision and answers the criticism with reasoned evidence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Wait 24–48 hours before reacting to strong responses.
2. Give customers time to use a new feature or live with a removal.
3. Separate loud negative voices from the majority response.
4. Do not reverse a necessary decision only because it is controversial.
5. Make the response after emotion settles and evidence improves.
