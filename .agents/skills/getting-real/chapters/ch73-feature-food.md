# Chapter 73: Feature Food

## Core Idea
New or interesting features can feed special-interest groups and generate product buzz. A small team can use a noteworthy technology to attract a community that already cares about that technology. The feature becomes a bridge between the product and an audience that may not notice ordinary promotion.

Use this advantage when the product already contains something new or notable. Do not add obscure technology only to create attention. Spotlight real work that gives people a reason to discuss the application.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Feature Food — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Present new or interesting features as useful material for communities that want to discuss them.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a feature has clear interest for developers, platform users, or another special-interest group.
  - **How**: Identify the audience that cares about the feature. Explain the real use of the technology. Spotlight it through the channels that serve that audience.
- **Technology Du Jour — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Ride the attention around a new or noteworthy technology when the product uses it for a real reason.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the application already uses a technology that has current public interest.
  - **How**: Show the feature and connect it to the product. Do not add an obscure technology only to gain notice.
- **Small-Team Speed Advantage — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use a small team’s ability to integrate new ideas before larger companies clear bureaucratic bottlenecks.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a small team can adopt a useful idea quickly.
  - **How**: Implement the idea rapidly, then explain the noteworthy result to its special-interest community.

## Key Concepts
- **Feature food**: A new or interesting feature that a community can discuss and share.
- **Special-interest group**: A community focused on a technology, platform, or subject.
- **Ruby on Rails**: The development platform that generated developer attention for Basecamp.
- **Ajax**: A web technology whose use helped Basecamp receive public attention.
- **RSS**: A feed format that attracted notice when Basecamp used it in a business application.
- **iCal integration**: A seemingly minor feature that reached Mac-related sites.
- **Bureaucratic bottleneck**: A large-company delay that slows adoption of new ideas.
- **Technology du jour**: A currently popular or noteworthy technology.

## Mental Models
- **Feature as signal**: A noteworthy feature signals that the product participates in a community’s current conversation.
- **Niche bridge**: One real integration can connect a product to a focused audience.
- **Spotlight, do not manufacture**: Promote technology already present for a useful reason instead of inventing novelty for attention.
- **Speed as leverage**: A small team can turn quick integration into both product value and public notice.

## Anti-patterns
- **Novelty theater**: Adding obscure technology only to gain attention creates work without durable product value.
- **Feature dumping**: Listing technical terms without showing their use gives the audience no useful story.
- **Audience mismatch**: Spotlighting a feature to people who do not care wastes the feature’s promotional potential.
- **Large-team delay**: Letting approval layers block useful ideas gives smaller teams the attention advantage.
- **Hype without use**: Riding technology attention without a real product use damages trust when people inspect the feature.

## Worked Example
Basecamp used Ruby on Rails and gained attention in the developer community. Its Ajax elements helped Business 2.0 name Basecamp a “key player in Ajax” beside Google, Yahoo, Microsoft, and Amazon. Basecamp’s RSS support drew notice because it was an early business use of RSS. Its iCal integration reached Mac-related sites that might not otherwise mention the application. Each example used a real feature as a bridge to a specific community.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify features that a special-interest group already wants to discuss.
2. Spotlight real technology use instead of adding novelty for publicity.
3. Use a small team’s speed to integrate useful ideas quickly.
4. Connect each feature to the audience that values it.
5. Explain the feature through its product use, not through jargon alone.
