# Chapter 3: Caveats, Disclaimers, and Other Preemptive Strikes

## Core Idea
Getting Real worked very well for its creators, but the authors do not present it as a universal system. Use judgment, add precautions for life- or finance-critical work, and adapt the principles to the size and structure of the organization.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Not all or nothing**: Use this rule when a project cannot adopt every Getting Real practice. Take the ideas that fit instead of rejecting the whole approach. For a weapons system, nuclear control plant, banking system for millions of customers, or another life- or finance-critical system, add the precautions that the risk requires.
- **Bold strokes with judgment and imagination**: Use clear, strong rules as a starting point. Stretch or break a rule when the situation demands it. The authors prefer provocative guidance to a vague statement that every answer depends on context.
- **Break up into smaller units**: Use this method inside a large company. Smaller units reduce the number of people involved and can work faster and better. Pitch a small project as a low-risk, low-investment proof of concept, then demonstrate results.
- **Go stealth**: Use this option when normal approval paths block a useful small experiment. Work under the radar, with a boss providing air cover when possible, and demonstrate results through small increments.
- **Just ship it**: Use the web's ability to change quickly. Ship when the core scenarios work, let customers show whether the idea is right, and fix minor bugs through later web updates.
- **Byte-size releases**: Ship fewer, quality features in pieces that users can digest. Avoid a large release that waits for every feature and every detail.
- **Foundations first, details during development**: Nail the foundations before development. Refine details during development instead of closing every open issue before work starts.

## Key Concepts
- **Additional precautions**: Extra controls needed for work where failure can harm life or finances.
- **Proof of concept**: A small project that tests a new concept and demonstrates real results.
- **Smaller unit**: A reduced team or project inside a larger company.
- **Air cover**: Support from a boss that lets a small team work without normal process pressure.
- **Core scenario**: A central user action that must work before release.
- **Customer feedback**: Direct evidence from users after the product ships.
- **Byte-size piece**: A small feature release that users can digest.
- **Customer connection**: The relationship formed by shipping, receiving feedback, and building what users want.

## Mental Models
- **Risk sets the precaution level**: Use a relaxed process only where the consequences permit it. Increase safeguards when life or finances depend on the system.
- **Small experiment before large approval**: Reduce a large organizational argument to a small project with measurable results.
- **The customer outranks internal agreement**: A shipped product and customer response provide stronger evidence than long internal meetings about the right thing.
- **Core first, polish later**: Release when core scenarios work, then correct minor bugs and refine the product through feedback.

## Anti-patterns
- **Universal application without risk judgment**: A laissez-faire attitude can fail in critical systems that need additional precautions.
- **All-or-nothing adoption**: Rejecting useful ideas because the whole system cannot fit blocks partial progress.
- **Claiming ownership of old ideas**: These techniques are not exclusive to Basecamp. Present them as the way the team works, not as inventions.
- **Large teams and process by default**: Too many people, meetings, and arguments delay action.
- **Months of planning and specifications**: Long preparation does not close the fundamental questions that real use can expose.
- **Big-bang releases**: Waiting for a whole new release delays feedback and makes mistakes more expensive to find.
- **Waiting for zero bugs**: Minor bugs should not block release after the core scenarios work. Fix them gradually on the web.

## Worked Example
The Start.com team applied Getting Real inside Microsoft. Rather than wait through months of feature planning and arguments, the team worked as a small unit. It did not ask permission for every step, had a boss who provided air cover, and took on a little at a time. The team shipped core scenarios, listened to users, and responded to feedback. Robert Scoble described this pattern as biting off a little at a time and doing it. Sanaz Ahari, Start.com's Program Manager, states the result as shipping, learning whether the idea works, and fixing it quickly on the web.

## Key Takeaways
1. Match process and precautions to the risk of the system.
2. Adopt useful parts of Getting Real when full adoption is not possible.
3. Split large-company work into smaller units and prove value with a small project.
4. Ship core scenarios instead of waiting for perfect specifications or a large release.
5. Let customer feedback settle questions that internal debate cannot answer.
6. Use strong rules as tools, then apply judgment when the situation changes.
