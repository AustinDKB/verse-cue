# Chapter 5: What's Your Problem?

## Core Idea
Build software for yourself by solving a problem you understand firsthand. Your own use gives you a clear target audience, better answers to daily product decisions, and the passion needed to keep improving the tool.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Build software for yourself**: Use this approach when you have a problem that blocks your own work. You know what matters, what does not, and what a useful solution must do. If the problem affects you, it may affect hundreds of thousands of other people too.
- **Scratching your own itch**: The Open Source world uses this phrase for building tools that developers want, delivered in the way they want. As your own user, you can answer many small product decisions from experience instead of guessing.
  - Dave Thomas, identified in the source as one of *The Pragmatic Programmers*, explains that developers who use their own tools know the correct answers to about 90 percent of these decisions.
- **Born out of necessity**: Use dissatisfaction with existing tools as a product signal. Build the solution you need instead of copying the options that already fail to solve the problem.
  - David Greiner used this approach for Campaign Monitor after existing email marketing tools each solved only part of the problem.
- **You need to care about it**: Choose work that holds personal meaning when you will live with the product for years. Malcolm Gladwell connects durable work with personal investment and the desire to tell the story.

## Key Concepts
- **Own problem**: A problem the builder experiences directly.
- **Target audience**: The people who share the problem and may use the solution.
- **Passion**: The care that makes the builder use, improve, and advocate for the tool.
- **Micro-decision**: A small product choice, such as a color, table, behavior, or recovery path.
- **Assumption debt**: The connected set of guesses that grows when the builder lacks direct knowledge.
- **Scratching your own itch**: Building a tool for a need the builder personally feels.
- **Necessity**: A strong reason to build that comes from a failure in available tools.
- **Personal investment**: The care that sustains work over a long period.

## Mental Models
- **User and builder in one person**: Treat personal use as a constant source of product information.
- **Guessing creates debt**: Every unanswered micro-decision can become an assumption linked to other assumptions.
- **Direct use reduces uncertainty**: When you are the user, experience answers many decisions before you need to ask others.
- **Need creates a market test**: A problem shared by many people can reveal a market without starting from an abstract market idea.
- **Passion supports persistence**: Care about the problem because long work needs more than an interesting idea.

## Anti-patterns
- **Building from an abstract market only**: A builder without a personal problem must guess more often about what matters.
- **Copying bloated tools**: Existing products may include billing, strict access controls, charts, graphs, and other features that do not solve the needed problem.
- **Ignoring personal use**: If the builder does not use or care about the tool, the product loses a strong source of knowledge and passion.
- **Accumulating assumptions**: Guessing through hundreds of small decisions creates an interconnected debt in the application.
- **Solving only part of the need**: A tool that handles some required functions but fails at another important function leaves the problem unsolved.

## Worked Example
Basecamp began at a design firm that needed a simple way to communicate with clients about projects. The firm maintained client extranets by changing HTML by hand, but the sites became stale and were abandoned. The available tools either failed to do what the firm needed or added unwanted features such as billing, strict access controls, charts, and graphs. The firm built its own focused tool instead. Because the team used and cared about the solution, it could make practical decisions and create a product that other people also wanted.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with a problem you personally understand.
2. Use your own product to answer daily decisions and reduce guessing.
3. Look for other people who share the same frustration.
4. Build from necessity instead of copying bloated tools.
5. Treat passion and personal investment as product resources.
6. Remember that a tool made for yourself can reach a larger audience.
