# Chapter 84: Keep the Posts Coming

## Core Idea
Keep a dedicated product blog active after launch. Frequent posts show that the product is alive, loved, and still in development. A quiet or abandoned blog suggests that the company has stopped listening or that nobody remains at the controls.

Post at least once a week, or more often when possible. Share FAQs, how-to guidance, tips and tricks, new features, updates, fixes, and product buzz. Use a friendly personal voice. A small team does not need to sound like a large corporation. It can talk to customers like a friend, link to competitors, discuss its philosophy, hint at future features, and leave comments open for feedback.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Living Product Blog — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use a continuing product blog to show active development after launch.
  - **When to use**: Use it from launch onward, especially when customers need evidence that the product still receives care.
  - **How**: Publish at least weekly. Cover FAQs, how-tos, tips, features, updates, fixes, and press. Keep the conversation active instead of issuing one announcement and disappearing.
- **Friendly Small-Team Voice — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Let the company sound human instead of imitating a large business.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a small team feels pressure to sound formal, distant, or ultra-professional.
  - **How**: Write in a friendly and personal tone. Revel in the ability to speak directly with customers.
- **Open Product Conversation — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Share information and invite customers into an ongoing exchange.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the company wants transparency, community, and loyalty rather than one-sided promotion.
  - **How**: State product philosophies. Link to and discuss competitors openly. Hint at upcoming features. Keep comments open so customers can give feedback.
- **Product Blog as Development Signal — Gina Trapani, web developer and editor of Lifehacker**: Treat regular product posts as evidence that an application has active people behind it.
  - **When to use**: Use it when customers or writers decide whether a web application remains active.
  - **How**: Keep the blog current and conversational. Give readers a reason to mention the product instead of sending only one-sided press releases.

## Key Concepts
- **Product blog**: A public channel for ongoing product and company communication.
- **Living product**: An application that continues to talk, listen, and develop.
- **Abandoned blog**: A quiet product channel that suggests stopped development.
- **How-to**: Guidance that shows customers how to use the product.
- **Tips and tricks**: Short pieces of practical product advice.
- **Product update**: A public account of a change or improvement.
- **Product buzz**: Public attention from press, writers, and customers.
- **Friendly voice**: Personal language that makes a small team feel human.
- **Open conversation**: Public exchange that includes company information and customer feedback.

## Mental Models
- **Blog as heartbeat**: Regular posts show that people still care for the product.
- **Conversation over broadcast**: An open exchange creates more trust than one-sided press releases.
- **Small is a communication advantage**: A small team can respond like a friend instead of hiding behind corporate language.

## Anti-patterns
- **Launch-only blog**: Stopping after launch makes the product look inactive.
- **One-sided press release**: Broadcasting news without conversation gives customers no way to respond.
- **Corporate imitation**: A stiff voice hides the direct human contact that makes a small team valuable.
- **Closed comments**: Removing feedback breaks the listening side of the product conversation.
- **Competitor silence**: Pretending competitors do not exist weakens open discussion and trust.

## Worked Example
A product team publishes at least one post each week. It rotates through FAQs, how-to guidance, tips, features, fixes, and press mentions. It writes in a friendly voice, links to competitors, hints at upcoming work, and keeps comments open. Gina Trapani described scanning active product blogs such as Google, Flickr, Yahoo, del.icio.us, and Basecamp, and said she was more likely to mention them than applications that sent isolated press releases.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep the product blog active after launch.
2. Post at least once each week when possible.
3. Mix help, updates, fixes, features, and product buzz.
4. Use a friendly small-team voice.
5. Keep information and customer feedback in the same conversation.
6. Treat blog activity as evidence that the product remains alive.
