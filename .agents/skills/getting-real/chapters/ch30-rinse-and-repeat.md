# Chapter 30: Rinse and Repeat

## Core Idea
Work in iterations instead of trying to get everything right the first time. A working application gives users and the team something to react to, so each cycle produces better decisions and a faster path to useful software.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rinse and repeat — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Treat product development as repeated design, use, analysis, and revision.
  - **When to use**: Use it for web software and for any screen or workflow that can improve through use.
  - **How**: Design a screen, use it in the active application, analyze what happens, and start another cycle. Let the application grow and change instead of protecting the first version.
- **Iterations lead to liberation — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use future revision to remove pressure from the first attempt.
  - **When to use**: Use it when fear of imperfection delays a useful idea.
  - **How**: Put the idea into a working form, learn whether it flies, and remember that a later pass can correct it. Do not demand final quality from the first pass.
- **Launch and revise — Seth Godin**: Keep the story straight, make the parts work, then improve after release.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team has a coherent product story but still sees unresolved details.
  - **How**: Make sure the pieces work, launch, and revise from real feedback. Use the combined intelligence of users and the team instead of relying on one person's prediction.

## Key Concepts
- **Iteration**: A repeated cycle that produces a better product decision.
- **Active application**: A working product that can produce real use and feedback.
- **Revision**: A later change based on what the current version teaches.
- **Real feedback**: Information from people using the software while change remains possible.
- **Human frailty**: The difficulty of imagining the effects of unseen actions.
- **Product story**: The clear explanation of what the product does and why it matters.
- **First attempt**: A useful version that does not need to be final.
- **Collective intelligence**: The stronger judgment that comes from many people reacting to a real result.

## Mental Models
- **Application as conversation**: Let the product speak through use, then answer with a revision.
- **Future pass as permission**: Knowing that another cycle will occur makes the first release easier to start.
- **Environment beats imagination**: People react well to visible conditions but predict unseen consequences poorly.
- **Launch as learning**: Release begins a feedback cycle rather than ending product work.

## Anti-patterns
- **First-pass perfection**: Delay release while trying to predict every consequence in advance.
- **Frozen design**: Treat the first screen as a final answer and refuse to learn from use.
- **Imaginary prioritization**: Rank unseen work without an active application to show what matters.
- **Launch without revision**: Release a product but ignore the feedback that could guide its next form.
- **Solo certainty**: Trust one person's intelligence more than the evidence produced by shared use.

## Worked Example
The team designs a screen and puts it into the active application. People use it, the team analyzes the result, and the team starts the screen again when the evidence shows a better path. The first version does not need perfection because the process already includes another pass. The team keeps the product story clear, makes its pieces work, launches, and revises while users can still show what requires attention.

## Key Takeaways
1. Put ideas into a working application early.
2. Repeat design, use, analysis, and revision.
3. Use later passes to reduce fear of an imperfect first version.
4. Launch when the product story and working pieces are clear.
5. Let real users guide the next iteration.
