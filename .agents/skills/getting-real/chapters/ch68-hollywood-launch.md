# Chapter 68: Hollywood Launch

## Core Idea
Launch in stages instead of releasing an application without an audience. Use a Hollywood-style sequence of **teaser**, **preview**, and **launch** to build awareness, anticipation, early use, and momentum.

The sequence creates a permission-based audience before release and gives early users a chance to test the product. Buzz helps only when the product delivers on its promise.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hollywood Launch — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Move from hints to previews to public release.
  - **When to use**: Use it when an application needs attention before launch day.
  - **How**: Start a teaser months ahead. Preview features weeks ahead. Launch to people who asked for news, then show progress.
- **Teaser — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Plant a clear but incomplete idea before the product is ready.
  - **When to use**: Use it months before launch, when the team can share the direction without revealing sensitive details.
  - **How**: Post a logo and vague blog news. Collect emails. Invite mavens and tastemakers. Links from Boing Boing, Slashdot, or Digg can bring traffic and PageRank.
- **Preview — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Give interested people enough detail to imagine the product and discuss its ideas.
  - **When to use**: Use it a few weeks before release, after the product has visible features or a clear theme.
  - **How**: Show screenshots, features, themes, and principles. Basecamp highlighted reminders and milestones, while Backpack posted its manifesto. Offer “golden tickets” and use early feedback without forcing praise.
- **Permission-Based Launch — Josh Williams, founder of Blinksale, from “The Road to Launch Day”**: Build a list of people who ask to hear about the product, then invite them through launch.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team can contact fans, friends, colleagues, and industry people.
  - **How**: Start with an existing list. Add a simple email form. Ask trusted people to spread it. Invite a small beta group, then email the list and let testers speak honestly.

## Key Concepts
- **Teaser**: An early hint that creates interest without showing everything.
- **Preview**: A pre-launch view of features, themes, principles, or screens.
- **Launch**: The public release when people can use the application.
- **Maven**: An informed early person who can notice and discuss a new product.
- **Tastemaker**: A person whose attention can influence wider awareness.
- **Golden ticket**: A limited early invitation that gives selected people access.
- **Beta tester**: An early user who tries the product before public release.
- **Permission list**: Email contacts who asked for product information.
- **Momentum**: Visible progress that keeps attention after launch.

## Mental Models
- **Staged reveal**: Give the audience more information as the product moves closer to use.
- **Permission funnel**: Move from a hint, to an email request, to a preview, to actual use.
- **Promise and delivery**: Build buzz only when the product can satisfy the attention it creates.

## Anti-patterns
- **Silent launch**: Releasing into a forest leaves no audience ready to listen or use the application.
- **No email capture**: Generating interest without a permission list loses the easiest launch audience.
- **Forced advocacy**: Telling beta testers to praise the product creates false buzz.
- **Clouds without rain**: Creating attention without a product that delivers turns anticipation into disappointment.

## Worked Example
Blinksale started with an existing permission list. Six weeks before launch, it posted a teaser for easier online invoicing. The page created suspense and asked only for an email. Friends and colleagues spread it through blogs, and thousands joined.

About two weeks before launch, Blinksale invited friends, colleagues, and industry mavens to beta test. The team wanted honest discussion, not forced writing. On launch day, it emailed the list, contacted blogging friends, and let testers speak. Tens of thousands visited, and thousands signed up.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use teaser, preview, and launch as separate stages.
2. Start an interest page and collect permission-based email addresses.
3. Give mavens and tastemakers an early reason to pay attention.
4. Show screenshots, features, themes, and principles during the preview.
5. Use a small beta group to learn and create honest discussion.
6. Email the interested list at launch and show progress afterward.
