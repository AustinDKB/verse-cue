# Chapter 10: Less Mass

## Core Idea
Reduce mass so the company and product can change direction with little energy. In web technology, change must stay easy and cheap because a team that cannot change on the fly loses ground to a team that can.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Less mass — Basecamp**: Use this framework when a product or company feels slow, heavy, or tied to old decisions.
  - **When to use**: Use it during team, process, technology, and roadmap decisions.
  - **How**: Remove long-term contracts, excess staff, permanent decisions, thick process, inventory, lock-ins, proprietary data formats, long-term roadmaps, and office politics. Prefer just-in-time thinking, multi-tasking team members, less software and code, fewer features, small teams, simplicity, open-source products, open data formats, and an open culture that permits mistakes.
- **Mass as change energy — Basecamp**: Use the physical-object analogy as a diagnostic. A more massive object needs more energy to change direction.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a proposed commitment may make future change expensive.
  - **How**: Ask what energy the commitment will need to reverse. Remove weight before it becomes part of the system.
- **Less-mass response — Basecamp**: Use this comparison when a new technology or concept appears.
  - **When to use**: Use it to test whether the current product can react to market demands.
  - **How**: Compare the team with less software and fewer features against the team with a larger product and a 12-month roadmap. Favor the team that can make the switch now.

## Key Concepts
- **Mass**: The commitments, structures, and product weight that resist change.
- **Long-term contract**: A commitment that limits future choices.
- **Permanent decision**: A choice treated as difficult or impossible to reverse.
- **Thick process**: A large procedure that slows action.
- **Technology lock-in**: Dependence on a technology that makes change costly.
- **Proprietary data format**: A closed format that limits movement and integration.
- **Just-in-time thinking**: Keeping decisions and resources near the time of need.
- **Open data format**: A format that keeps data easier to use elsewhere.
- **Organic process**: A process that responds to current needs instead of a long roadmap.
- **Change direction**: Move the product, business model, priorities, or message toward new evidence.

## Mental Models
- **Cigarette boat versus aircraft carrier**: Prefer a small, steerable operation when the environment changes often.
- **Weight predicts delay**: Each commitment adds energy to the next change.
- **Open choices preserve speed**: Open formats, simple systems, and reversible decisions keep options available.
- **Change of mind as strength**: Treat a fast correction as an advantage, not as failure.

## Anti-patterns
- **Long-term commitments by default**: They make a new direction expensive to adopt.
- **Excess staff and process**: More coordination paths and approvals slow a response.
- **Long-term roadmaps**: A roadmap can keep the team discussing a change after a lighter team has made it.
- **Proprietary lock-in**: Closed technology and data formats restrict integration and movement.
- **The past ruling the future**: Old decisions can force current work even when evidence changes.
- **Office politics**: Internal power contests consume energy that should support product change.

## Worked Example
Imagine two companies when Ajax or tagging becomes useful. One has less software, fewer features, and an organic process that focuses on current needs. The other has more software, more features, and a 12-month roadmap. The less-mass company can adapt to real market demand first. The heavier company may still discuss the change or push it through bureaucracy after the lighter company has switched.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure commitments by the change energy they will require.
2. Keep software, features, teams, and processes small.
3. Prefer open formats and open-source products when they preserve options.
4. Avoid permanent decisions when a reversible choice is sufficient.
5. Replace long roadmaps with attention to current needs.
6. Treat fast correction and a change of mind as product strengths.
