# Chapter 83: One Month Tuneup

## Core Idea
Issue a major update about 30 days after launch. A fast, meaningful update shows momentum, proves that the team listens, creates a second wave of attention, and gives customers and writers something new to discuss.

A planned tuneup also improves the first launch. The team can perfect the core feature set instead of squeezing in every extra idea before release. After launch, real customers show which areas need attention. The team then uses that feedback to choose the next improvement.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **One Month Tuneup — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Plan a major update 30 days after the initial release.
  - **When to use**: Use it after launching a product with a focused, useful core.
  - **How**: Set the next update close enough to maintain momentum. Use early customer feedback to choose meaningful additions or repairs. Release the update as a second product moment.
- **Baby-Step Launch — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Launch the core first and grow the product through small, informed steps.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team feels pressure to add more features before customers can use the product.
  - **How**: Perfect the crucial components. Release them. Let the product operate in the real world. Use customer feedback to select the next area of work.
- **Second Wave of Buzz — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use a major update to renew attention after launch.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the first launch conversation begins to fade.
  - **How**: Give customers a new reason to talk. Give writers and bloggers a new event to cover. Reaffirm the good feeling from the first release.

## Key Concepts
- **Major update**: A substantial product change that creates a new public event.
- **30 days**: The chapter’s rule for the first post-launch tuneup.
- **Momentum**: Evidence that the product continues to move forward.
- **Core feature set**: The crucial capabilities that make the initial product useful.
- **Real world**: Actual customer use after launch.
- **Customer feedback**: Information that shows which product areas need attention.
- **Second wave of buzz**: New public attention created by a post-launch update.
- **Baby-step approach**: A launch method that grows the product through small releases.
- **Initial good feelings**: Positive customer response that a later update can reaffirm.

## Mental Models
- **Launch is a beginning**: The first release starts product learning instead of ending product work.
- **A short clock focuses scope**: A planned update makes it easier to stop adding nonessential features before launch.
- **Customers reveal the next step**: Real use provides better priorities than prelaunch guesses.
- **Updates are communication**: A major update shows listening and creates a fresh reason for public discussion.

## Anti-patterns
- **Prelaunch squeeze**: Adding a few more features before launch delays the core product and weakens focus.
- **One-shot launch**: Treating the first release as the only product event wastes the chance to build momentum.
- **Guess-based roadmap**: Choosing post-launch work without real customer feedback ignores the reason for releasing early.
- **No update promise**: Failing to plan a quick improvement can reduce urgency and public attention.
- **Huge first scope**: Trying to include every desired capability prevents the team from learning from actual use.

## Worked Example
Backpack launched with its base product first. A few weeks later, the team added Backpack Mobile for handheld devices and tagging because customers identified those capabilities as important. The initial launch stayed focused, while the follow-up update showed momentum and answered real customer demand.

## Key Takeaways
1. Plan a major update about 30 days after launch.
2. Perfect the core feature set instead of delaying for extras.
3. Let real customer use select the next work.
4. Use the update to show momentum and listening.
5. Treat a follow-up release as a second wave of useful attention.
