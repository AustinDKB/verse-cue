# Chapter 38: Meetings Are Toxic

## Core Idea
Meetings break the workday into small pieces and often exchange few useful facts. Avoid them when a simpler explanation can support email, instant messaging, or Campfire, and hold a meeting only for an important issue that needs input, approval, or agreement.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Avoid meetings — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Simplify unclear concepts instead of using a meeting as the first response.
  - **When to use**: Use it when people suggest a meeting because the subject feels difficult to explain.
  - **How**: Clarify the concept and discuss it through email, instant messaging, or Campfire. Treat every avoided meeting minute as available work time.
- **Rare meeting protocol — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Limit a necessary meeting with time, people, and purpose.
  - **When to use**: Use it only when an important business issue needs input, approval, or agreement.
  - **How**: Set a 30-minute timer, invite as few people as possible, and write a clear agenda. End when the timer rings.
- **Fewer meetings — Lisa Haneberg**: Push back on meetings that lack a clear purpose or produce no useful result.
  - **When to use**: Use it when someone invites people without a necessary decision or asks for attendance from everyone.
  - **How**: Ask what important issue needs discussion and whose input, approval, or agreement matters. Decline the rest.
- **Small autonomous units — The Ganssle Group**: Reduce communication links by dividing teams and programs into smaller independent units.
  - **When to use**: Use it as projects grow and communication begins to consume the workday.
  - **How**: Partition teams and programs. Minimize dependencies between units so memos, meetings, shared data, and shared hardware do not dominate the work.

## Key Concepts
- **Meeting**: A group discussion that consumes shared work time.
- **Meeting toxicity**: The productivity loss caused by fragmented time and low information flow.
- **Clear agenda**: A stated subject that tells participants why they meet.
- **30-minute timer**: The hard limit for a necessary meeting.
- **Input**: Information needed for an important business issue.
- **Approval**: A decision that authorizes a course of action.
- **Agreement**: Shared acceptance needed to move forward.
- **Communication link**: A possible exchange path between workers.
- **Autonomous unit**: A small team or program part with few dependencies.
- **Dependency**: A connection that requires one unit to coordinate with another.

## Mental Models
- **Meeting as last resort**: First make the subject clear enough for written communication.
- **Communication-link growth**: Two workers have one path, three have three, and four have six. More people create more paths to coordinate.
- **Small-unit control**: Smaller independent teams and program parts reduce communication overhead.
- **Timer as boundary**: A fixed end time forces the group to focus on the important issue.

## Anti-patterns
- **Meeting by habit**: A meeting replaces clear writing and consumes time without solving the unclear concept.
- **Vague agenda**: Participants cannot prepare or decide because nobody knows the meeting subject.
- **Large invitation list**: Extra people add communication cost and waste time.
- **Open-ended meeting**: Without a hard stop, discussion drifts away from the issue.
- **Dependency web**: Shared data, shared hardware, and tightly linked program parts make every change require more coordination.
- **Meeting preparation gap**: A meeting needs preparation that participants often do not complete, so the group starts without useful material.
- **Abstract discussion**: Meetings often discuss words instead of real code or interface design, so they provide little concrete information.
- **Unproductive participant**: One person can waste the group’s time with nonsense, even when the subject matters.

## Worked Example
A team faces a difficult product question. First, it simplifies the question and discusses it through email, instant messaging, or Campfire. If the issue still needs input, approval, or agreement, the organizer writes a clear agenda, invites only the required people, and starts a 30-minute timer. The meeting ends when the timer rings. As the project grows, the team divides into small autonomous units and reduces dependencies, so fewer communication links need meetings.

## Key Takeaways
1. Simplify a concept before scheduling a meeting.
2. Hold a meeting only for an important issue that needs input, approval, or agreement.
3. Set a 30-minute limit and end on time.
4. Invite the fewest people who can decide or contribute.
5. Reduce communication links with small autonomous units.
