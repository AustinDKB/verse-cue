# Chapter 35: Shrink Your Time

## Core Idea
Long estimates hide unknown work and invite delay. Break large timeframes and problems into small, concrete pieces that people can understand, finish, and revise.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Shrink your time — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Replace a distant estimate with smaller time units.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a project estimate spans weeks or months, or when a problem feels too large to understand.
  - **How**: Divide the timeframe and the problem again and again. Turn a 12-week project into twelve one-week projects. Turn a task longer than 30 hours into 6-to-10-hour chunks, then take one step at a time.
- **Smaller tasks and smaller timelines — Gina Trapani**: Give work a narrow target and a short deadline.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a task invites procrastination, hides requirements, or makes the team forget earlier agreements.
  - **How**: Define a small, specific module that fits an afternoon or another short period. Finish it, learn from it, and move to the next module.
- **True factors — Merlin Mann**: State “I do not know” when a precise answer cannot exist yet.
  - **When to use**: Use it when someone asks for an exact deadline, cost, or quantity that current knowledge cannot support.
  - **How**: Say “I don't know” first. Ask how exact the estimate must be and why. Work together to identify the true factors behind the number.
- **Solve the one problem staring you in the face — Andre Torrez**: Solve the immediate useful problem before designing a broad answer.
  - **When to use**: Use it when future problems create committees, specifications, or unnecessary complexity.
  - **How**: Identify the next clear problem, make the smallest effective solution, and release it. Torrez’s “nofollow” attribute shows this simple response. Do not trade a swift local result for a plan that solves twenty hypothetical problems.

## Key Concepts
- **Timeframe**: The period assigned to a project or task.
- **Small chunk**: A work unit that fits a realistic short period.
- **Specific module**: A narrow piece of work with a clear result.
- **Estimate**: A forecast that remains uncertain until work exposes its factors.
- **True factor**: A condition that affects a number or decision.
- **Unknown**: Information that prevents a responsible exact answer.
- **Procrastination**: Delay that large, vague tasks make easy.
- **Requirement misunderstanding**: A mismatch hidden by a long task period.
- **Immediate problem**: The next concrete issue that needs a useful solution.
- **Nofollow attribute**: Andre Torrez’s example of a simple, effective response to an immediate web problem.

## Mental Models
- **Telescope reversal**: Move from a distant project view to the next short segment.
- **Small-task filter**: Use short work units to expose misunderstandings before they become expensive.
- **Honest uncertainty**: Replace false precision with a shared search for the factors that matter.
- **Problem in front**: Solve the visible, current problem before preparing for a long list of imagined problems.

## Anti-patterns
- **Month-long fantasy estimate**: A distant forecast pretends to know events that have not happened.
- **Large vague task**: A broad assignment invites delay and hides requirement errors.
- **False precision**: An exact number sounds credible but ignores unknown factors.
- **Future-problem committee**: Discussion of hypothetical needs turns a small fix into a slow specification.
- **Overbuilding**: A team solves many possible problems instead of the one that blocks current progress.

## Worked Example
A 12-week project becomes twelve one-week projects. A task that appears to need more than 30 hours becomes several 6-to-10-hour chunks. Instead of giving a programmer three weeks for a large assignment, give an afternoon to a small, specific module. With three weeks, a programmer can spend two and a half weeks delaying and one week coding against misunderstood requirements. The shorter task reduces coordination and misunderstanding, gives an earlier result, and makes a change cheaper.

## Key Takeaways
1. Break long projects into one-week or smaller units.
2. Break tasks longer than 30 hours into 6-to-10-hour chunks.
3. Say “I do not know” when the facts do not support precision.
4. Ask what accuracy the decision actually needs.
5. Solve the next concrete problem before hypothetical future problems.
