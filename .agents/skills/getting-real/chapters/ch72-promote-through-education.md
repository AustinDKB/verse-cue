# Chapter 72: Promote Through Education

## Core Idea
Share knowledge with the community instead of relying on a hard sell. Teaching people about a useful subject gives them a service, builds goodwill, and places the product and team in front of people who may not notice an advertisement. People who learn from the team can become product evangelists.

Education works as promotion because it helps first. The product can remain present through the subject, examples, and tools, but the public material must stand on its own as useful knowledge. This approach pays forward the support that the community gives the product.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Promote Through Education — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use teaching as a soft way to create attention and positive buzz.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team has knowledge that can help customers, practitioners, or the wider community.
  - **How**: Publish tips and tricks. Speak at conferences and meet attendees afterward. Run workshops. Give interviews. Write helpful articles or books. Teach a method related to the product without turning the lesson into a sales pitch.
- **Pay It Forward — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, Matthew Linderman, and David Greiner**: Share knowledge that helps customers do better work, because their success can create more success for the product.
  - **When to use**: Use it when customers can improve their own service, results, or business through practical guidance.
  - **How**: Share articles and tips freely. Help customers provide a better service to their customers. Build expert status, strengthen customer relationships, and receive targeted inbound traffic from search engines and bloggers.
## Key Concepts
- **Education**: Useful teaching that helps people understand or do something.
- **Soft promotion**: Attention earned through service instead of a direct purchase request.
- **Goodwill**: Positive trust created by helping the community.
- **Evangelist**: A person who shares support for a product with others.
- **Tips and tricks**: Short practical lessons that readers can apply.
- **Yellow Fade Technique**: A method for subtly spotlighting a recently changed area on a page.
- **Open source**: Development infrastructure shared so a community can inspect, use, and improve it.
- **Inbound traffic**: Visitors who arrive through search engines, articles, or shared links.
- **Pay it forward**: Help customers first so the resulting success benefits more people.

## Mental Models
- **Teacher before seller**: Give the audience a useful lesson before asking for product attention.
- **Knowledge flywheel**: Teaching creates trust, trust creates sharing, and sharing brings more relevant people to the product.
- **Customer-success loop**: When customers improve their service, they can win more business, which can create more business for the product.
- **Public lesson as evidence**: A useful method demonstrates the team’s competence without claiming competence directly.

## Anti-patterns
- **Hard-sell lesson**: A lesson that exists only to say “buy this product” removes the service that makes education credible.
- **Knowledge hoarding**: Keeping useful methods private loses goodwill, search traffic, and community learning.
- **Empty expertise claim**: Calling the team expert without teaching anything gives people no evidence.
- **Promotion without help**: Mentioning the product while ignoring the audience’s problem turns education into advertising.
- **Contribution without community care**: Sharing a tool but ignoring feedback and contributions misses the reciprocal value of open source.

## Worked Example
The authors wrote about the **Yellow Fade Technique**, a method for subtly highlighting a changed page area. The post taught a useful lesson, attracted thousands of page views, and exposed readers to the authors’ products. During Ruby on Rails development, the team made the infrastructure open source. That choice gave back to the community, built goodwill and recognition, brought useful feedback, and produced patches and contributions. David Greiner gives a related Campaign Monitor example: free articles and tips about email marketing help customers provide better service, gain more business, and send targeted inbound traffic back to the company.

## Key Takeaways
1. Teach a useful subject instead of starting with a hard product pitch.
2. Publish tips, articles, interviews, workshops, and talks in forms people can share.
3. Help customers improve their own work and business.
4. Use open source when a real contribution can help the community.
5. Accept feedback and contributions as part of giving back.
6. Let goodwill, expertise, and inbound traffic follow the value of the lesson.
