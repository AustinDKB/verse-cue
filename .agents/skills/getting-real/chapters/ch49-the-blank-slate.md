# Chapter 49: The Blank Slate

## Core Idea
Design the blank slate as a first impression, not as a missing version of the regular screen. New customers judge the application before they have data, so the first-run experience must explain the page, show its future value, and give a clear starting point.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Thoughtful blank slate — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use the empty first-run screen to set expectations and reduce confusion.
  - **When to use**: Use it whenever a new customer opens a screen before entering posts, links, comments, hours, sidebar information, or other data.
  - **How**: Add a short tutorial or help text, show a sample populated screen, explain how to start, describe the eventual screen, and answer the first questions.
- **First-run experience — John Gruber**: Treat the first part of the product experience as the most important part of the whole experience.
  - **When to use**: Use it when deciding where to spend design time during setup and initial use.
  - **How**: Give unusual care to the first one-thousandth because it sets the customer’s expectations and first impression.
- **Blank-state expectation setting — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use the absence of data as a guide instead of a dead end.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the regular screen looks useful only after customer data exists.
  - **How**: Tell the customer what the page is, what to do now, and how the screen will look after the customer adds data.

## Key Concepts
- **Blank slate**: The empty screen a new customer sees before entering data.
- **First-run experience**: The first use that shapes expectations about the application.
- **First impression**: The customer’s early judgment of the product’s value and clarity.
- **Sample screenshot**: A picture that shows how a populated screen will look.
- **Help blurb**: Short guidance that explains a page or next action.
- **Expectation**: The customer’s idea of what the application will do and require.
- **Populated screen**: A screen filled with the data that gives the application its normal shape.
- **Test data blindness**: Failure to notice the blank slate because development work always uses data.
- **False impression**: A negative judgment caused by an empty screen rather than by the product’s true value.

## Mental Models
- **First-thousandth rule**: The smallest time share can have the largest effect when it comes first.
- **Empty-to-useful path**: A blank screen should show the steps from no data to a useful product.
- **Expectation bridge**: Use guidance and a sample view to connect the customer’s empty screen to its future form.
- **Customer-eye test**: Review the application as a new customer instead of as a developer with test data.

## Anti-patterns
- **Data-filled-only design**: A polished screen with test data says nothing about the first real visit.
- **Silent empty screen**: A blank page leaves the customer unsure what the page means or what to do.
- **Developer test-data blindness**: Repeated use with sample records hides the state most customers see first.
- **First-run neglect**: Saving the setup experience for later creates a negative and false impression.
- **Expectation gap**: Showing no future result makes customers unable to see why they should continue.

## Worked Example
A new customer opens an empty screen for posts, links, comments, hours, or sidebar information. Instead of showing a silent blank page, the team explains what the page is, gives the first step, shows a sample screenshot with data, and describes the finished view. The customer can see what is missing, what to do next, and why the page will become useful after entry.

## Key Takeaways
1. Design the blank slate before relying on test data.
2. Explain what the empty page is and what to do next.
3. Show a sample of the populated result.
4. Set expectations for the future screen.
5. Use first-run work to reduce frustration and intimidation.
6. Treat the first impression as a high-value design task.
