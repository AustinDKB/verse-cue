# Chapter 36: Unity

## Core Idea
Separate departments lose the context of the whole web application. Keep design, development, copywriting, support, and marketing in active contact so information moves directly and the product stays coherent.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Unity, do not split into silos — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Integrate specialties instead of placing them in isolated silos.
  - **When to use**: Use it when departments work on one product but see only their own part of the work.
  - **How**: Create regular back-and-forth dialogue across specialties. Keep the whole application visible during development.
- **Checks and balances — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Let one specialty check another before errors become product problems.
  - **When to use**: Use it when translation between teams can remove context or hide customer information.
  - **How**: Let copywriters work with designers. Make support queries visible to developers. Use direct contact to catch lost meaning.
- **Multiple talents — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Prefer people who can contribute across more than one specialty when possible.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a small team needs shared context during product development.
  - **How**: Hire and support people who can wear different hats. Let their combined skills reduce handoffs and support a more harmonious product.

## Key Concepts
- **Unity**: Shared product context across specialties.
- **Silo**: An isolated department that sees only its own work.
- **Specialization**: Focus on one skill or product area.
- **Back-and-forth dialogue**: Direct exchange that lets teams correct work together.
- **Checks and balances**: Cross-team review that catches lost context.
- **Handoff**: Transfer of work from one specialty to another.
- **Multiple talents**: Skills that let one person contribute in different roles.
- **Product harmony**: Coherent work created through integrated contributions.

## Mental Models
- **Whole-product view**: Judge each contribution in the context of the full application.
- **Context bridge**: Use direct collaboration to preserve meaning across specialties.
- **Hat-sharing**: Treat roles as useful skills, not walls between people.

## Anti-patterns
- **Department silos**: Isolated work hides how one decision affects the rest of the application.
- **Translation chain**: Long handoffs lose customer context and create avoidable errors.
- **Single-skill wall**: Refusing cross-role work prevents useful checks and shared understanding.

## Worked Example
A copywriter works directly with a designer instead of sending text through an isolated department. A support query reaches a developer instead of stopping at support. The teams then use the customer context to adjust the interface and the product behavior together. This small exchange demonstrates the chapter’s checks and balances without adding another translation layer.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep specialties connected during product work.
2. Make customer support information visible to developers.
3. Let copywriters and designers review work together.
4. Value people who can contribute across roles.
5. Judge work by the whole application, not one department’s view.
