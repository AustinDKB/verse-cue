# Chapter 74: Track Your Logs

## Core Idea
Study logs and public references to learn who talks about the product and where its buzz comes from. Inbound links, criticism, and praise all show how people experience the application. A team should respond as a human, thank people who help, invite interested writers to future news, and use trustworthy praise in promotion.

Negative comments also deserve attention. A thoughtful answer can show that the team listens, explain a deliberate choice, and turn a critic into an evangelist. Statistics can show a trouble spot, but they cannot replace the customer voice.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Track Your Logs — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use traffic and reference logs to find the people and places shaping product buzz.
  - **When to use**: Use it whenever the application receives links, blog mentions, praise, or criticism.
  - **How**: Find who links to the product and which blogs list it. Visit active sources. Leave comments, thank people for links, and ask whether they want an advance list for future releases and updates.
- **Praise-to-Buzz — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Turn credible third-party praise into public evidence.
  - **When to use**: Use it when customers or reviewers describe the application in positive terms.
  - **How**: Collect useful praise and create a buzz page. Prefer third-party praise because people usually trust it more than a company’s own claims.
- **Thoughtful Critique Response — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Respond to negative feedback in a way that acknowledges the person and explains the product decision.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a public comment raises a complaint or a valid concern.
  - **How**: Show that the team listens. Explain the reason for the choice, or state that the team is working on the point. Keep the response thoughtful and human.

## Key Concepts
- **Log**: A record that helps show traffic and public references.
- **Buzz**: Public discussion and attention around the application.
- **Inbound link**: A link from another site to the product.
- **Advance list**: People who ask to hear about future releases or updates.
- **Third-party praise**: Positive comments from people outside the product team.
- **Testimonial**: A customer or reviewer statement used as evidence.
- **Critique**: Feedback that identifies a problem or disagreement.
- **Evangelist**: A critic or user who becomes an active supporter.

## Mental Models
- **Listening radar**: Logs show where the product enters public conversation.
- **Praise as evidence**: Outside words can carry more trust than company claims.
- **Criticism as contact**: A complaint gives the team a chance to show care and explain context.
- **Voice over numbers**: Statistics locate a problem, but direct comments explain the human experience.

## Anti-patterns
- **Silent monitoring**: Finding a link but never acknowledging its author wastes a relationship opportunity.
- **Praise hoarding**: Collecting positive comments without making useful evidence visible limits their value.
- **Defensive dismissal**: Rejecting a complaint without listening hardens the critic.
- **Statistics-only listening**: Treating logs as a replacement for customer voices hides the reason behind the numbers.
- **Generic reply**: An impersonal response fails to show that a real person understands the concern.

## Worked Example
A team finds a blog that links to the application. It thanks the writer in a comment and asks whether the writer wants to join the advance list. It saves a strong customer statement for a buzz page. If another post is negative, the team replies, “We appreciate the feedback but we did it this way because…” or, when the point is valid, “You raise a good point and we’re working on it.” The response acknowledges the person and can soften the critic.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check logs to find links, praise, and criticism.
2. Thank people who link to the application.
3. Invite interested writers to an advance list.
4. Use third-party praise as trustworthy promotional evidence.
5. Answer criticism thoughtfully and explain the product context.
6. Listen to voices instead of relying on statistics alone.
