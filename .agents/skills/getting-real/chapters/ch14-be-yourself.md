# Chapter 14: Be Yourself

## Core Idea
Use small-company size as a communication advantage. Speak directly, use familiar language, show a human voice, and tell customers the truth instead of imitating a large company.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Be yourself — Basecamp**: Use this approach when a small company competes with a larger one or feels pressure to look bigger.
  - **When to use**: Use it in customer communication, product language, and internal work.
  - **How**: Remove formalities and unnecessary bureaucracy. Talk with customers instead of down to them. Let people speak openly and honestly without many processes or sign-offs.
- **Proudly, defiantly truthful — Khoi Vinh**: Use this rule when describing company size, staff, or service breadth.
  - **When to use**: Use it whenever exaggeration seems likely to make the company look safer or larger.
  - **How**: State the exact size and breadth of the company. Do not use white lies. Smart customers learn the truth, and honesty supports meaningful, lasting, mutually beneficial relationships.
- **Any time at all — Edward Knittel**: Use direct access as a customer-service advantage when a small team can answer personally.
  - **When to use**: Use it when customers need a clear way to ask questions or report problems.
  - **How**: Publish a toll-free number that forwards to mobile phones. List mobile numbers on business cards. Tell customers that they can make contact at any time, regardless of the problem.

## Key Concepts
- **Small-company advantage**: Direct communication created by size and proximity to customers.
- **Formalities**: Procedures and behavior that add distance without adding value.
- **Bureaucracy**: Organizational process that slows direct action.
- **Human voice**: Familiar, personal language in the product and company.
- **Corporate drone**: Impersonal language that hides the people behind the company.
- **Truthful size**: An accurate statement of staff and service breadth.
- **Direct access**: A clear path from the customer to a real team member.
- **Customer trust**: Confidence created by honest information and reliable contact.
- **Sign-off**: Formal approval that can slow a small team.

## Mental Models
- **Small as close**: Treat size as a shorter distance to customers, not as a weakness.
- **Human voice as interface**: Use familiar language to reduce distance and make the company easier to understand.
- **Truth compounds**: Accurate claims support relationships that can last; exaggeration fails when customers learn the facts.
- **Access builds trust**: A direct contact path shows that the company accepts responsibility for the customer’s problem.

## Anti-patterns
- **Acting big**: Imitating large-company behavior gives up the communication advantage of being small.
- **Corporate jargon**: Complex or impersonal language talks down to customers and hides meaning.
- **Formal process by default**: Multiple sign-offs and arduous procedures slow ideas and service.
- **Exaggerating staff or offerings**: Smart customers discover the truth and lose the basis for a lasting relationship.
- **Inaccessible support**: A hidden or distant contact path weakens trust and prevents quick help.

## Worked Example
Edward Knittel described KennelSource’s support model. The company listed a toll-free number that forwarded to the team’s mobile phones, and each business card listed a mobile number. The team told customers that they could make contact at any time about any problem. Customers valued the trust, and no one abused the service.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use small size to communicate directly with customers.
2. Replace jargon with familiar, human language.
3. Remove formalities and extra approval steps.
4. State the exact size and breadth of the company.
5. Provide a direct contact path when the team can support it.
6. Treat honesty and access as product and service advantages.
