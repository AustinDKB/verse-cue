# Chapter 80: Tough Love

## Core Idea
The customer is not always right about the product. A development company must filter requests instead of adding every requested feature. If the team obeys every customer wish, the product becomes large, inconsistent, and opposite to the simplicity that customers may value most.

The team must sometimes say no. A coherent product can serve the larger customer group better than one assembled from every request.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Tough Love — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Consider customer requests without treating them as commands.
  - **When to use**: Use it whenever a request adds complexity, conflicts with the product’s direction, or serves a small group at a high cost.
  - **How**: Listen to the request. Check its effect on simplicity, time, and the wider customer base. Say no when the request does not improve the product for the people it should serve.
- **Act as a Filter — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Make product decisions instead of transferring every decision to the customer.
  - **When to use**: Use it when surveys, support messages, or feature lists contain competing demands.
  - **How**: Treat requests as input. Compare them with the product’s purpose and the needs of the larger group. Keep only the changes that fit the product.
- **93 Percent over 7 Percent — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Choose the larger product benefit when a narrow compatibility cost is not worth the work.
  - **When to use**: Use it when support for one platform or group consumes time that could improve the product for most customers.
  - **How**: Estimate the affected group and the work required. Basecamp declined Internet Explorer 5 support even though that choice wrote off 7 percent of the market, because the team preferred to improve the product for the other 93 percent.
- **Product Love — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Protect the team’s ability to respect and enjoy the product it makes.
  - **When to use**: Use it when requested additions would fill the product with things the team does not believe belong.
  - **How**: Veto unnecessary requests. Keep the product aligned with the team’s standards so the result remains a product the team can love.

## Key Concepts
- **Feature request**: A customer suggestion for a product capability.
- **Filter**: A product decision process that keeps some requests and rejects others.
- **Simplicity**: The product quality that can disappear when every request becomes a feature.
- **Customer survey**: A source of customer preference that still needs product judgment.
- **Market share**: The portion of the market affected by a support decision.
- **Veto**: A deliberate decision not to add a proposed capability.
- **Product love**: The team’s belief that the product contains the right things.

## Mental Models
- **Customer input is not product authority**: Requests reveal needs, but the team must decide the right product response.
- **Whole-product coherence beats feature accumulation**: A smaller product that fits together can serve more people than a broad product without a clear shape.
- **Opportunity cost is a customer decision**: Time spent on a narrow request cannot improve the product for the wider group.

## Anti-patterns
- **Customer-is-always-right thinking**: Treating every request as correct produces a product nobody wants to use.
- **Feature pile-up**: Adding comprehensive time tracking, billing, meetings, calendars, dependencies, chat, and wiki functions destroys a simple product.
- **Small-group dominance**: Spending too much time on a narrow platform can harm the larger customer group.
- **No product veto**: Refusing to reject unnecessary requests fills the product with capabilities the team does not support.

## Worked Example
Basecamp received requests for comprehensive time tracking, billing, meeting scheduling, calendaring, dependency systems, instant messaging, wiki functions, and more. Yet the number-one survey request was to keep Basecamp simple. The team therefore filtered the requests instead of adding them. It made the same choice with Internet Explorer 5, giving up 7 percent of the market to improve the product for the other 93 percent.

## Key Takeaways
1. Listen to every request without obeying every request.
2. Test proposed features against simplicity and product direction.
3. Compare work cost with the number of customers who benefit.
4. Say no when a request damages the whole product.
5. Protect the team’s ability to love and support its own product.
