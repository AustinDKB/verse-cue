# Chapter 18: Hire the Right Customers

## Core Idea
The customer is not always right for a particular product. Find the core market, focus the product and message on that group, and let the right customers guide useful decisions. A narrow audience can create stronger adoption than a broad promise.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hire the right customers — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use a defined core market as the product's first audience.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team tries to please every possible customer or cannot decide which requests matter.
  - **How**: Identify who the application is really intended for. Build and market for that group. Treat its shared needs as stronger evidence than requests from unrelated users.
- **If you try to please everyone, you will please no one — Basecamp team**: Use this rule when broad appeal begins to weaken the product.
  - **When to use**: Use it when feature choices conflict or the message becomes generic.
  - **How**: Choose the people most likely to care, make their work easier, and leave out features that serve only a distant audience.
- **Focused community — David Greiner, Campaign Monitor**: Use a tightly defined audience to simplify product decisions and spread the word.
  - **When to use**: Use it after the team identifies a group with similar needs and places where that group already gathers.
  - **How**: Advertise where the audience spends time online. Publish useful articles for that audience. Build a community around the product and use common needs to decide what to include or leave out.

## Key Concepts
- **Core market**: The specific group the application is intended to serve first.
- **Right customer**: A customer whose needs fit the product's vision and strengths.
- **Market focus**: Concentration of product and marketing effort on one audience.
- **Shared need**: A repeated problem among members of the core market.
- **Feature filter**: A market-based test for keeping or removing a feature.
- **Passionate customer**: A customer with enough interest to advocate for the product.
- **Evangelize**: Spread strong support for a product to other people.
- **Community**: A group connected by a shared product need or interest.

## Mental Models
- **Market as a filter**: Use the core market to decide which requests deserve product attention.
- **Narrow first, spread later**: A clear audience makes a product easier to explain and recommend.
- **Shared needs reduce noise**: Similar customers give more consistent evidence than a crowd with unrelated goals.
- **Customer fit over customer volume**: A smaller group that values the product can guide better decisions than many indifferent users.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pleasing everyone**: A universal product loses a clear purpose and satisfies no group deeply.
- **Treating every customer as equally right**: A request from outside the core market can distort the product.
- **Generic marketing**: A broad message gives no particular group a reason to care.
- **Building unrelated features**: Features for distant audiences increase work and weaken the product's center.
- **Ignoring existing communities**: The team wastes a natural path for education, trust, and product adoption.

## Worked Example
Basecamp focused its marketing on design firms. That choice attracted passionate customers who could evangelize the product and gave the team a clear audience for product decisions. David Greiner made the same choice with Campaign Monitor by targeting the web design market. The focus made useful features easy to identify and made it easier to leave out features that web designers did not need. It also supported targeted advertising, relevant articles, and a product community.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name the core market before broadening the product.
2. Use shared customer needs as a feature filter.
3. Build for customers who fit the product's vision.
4. Market where the core audience already spends time.
5. Let a focused community help spread and improve the product.
