# Chapter 8: Have an Enemy

## Core Idea
Define what your product opposes. An enemy clarifies what the product should not do, gives it direction, and creates a story customers understand quickly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pick a fight — Basecamp**: Use this when direction feels vague or a large competitor defines the category. Choose the tool or approach that represents what you reject.
  - **When to use**: Use it before feature selection and product messaging.
  - **How**: Name the enemy, identify its behavior, and build the opposite into the product. Use the enemy as a boundary, not a feature list.
- **The anti-Project — Basecamp**: Use Microsoft Project as the contrast when project management risks becoming charts, graphs, reports, statistics, and a project manager broadcasting a plan.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a category leader makes the product seem more complex or controlling than the user problem requires.
  - **How**: Reframe project management as communication and shared responsibility. Make everyone, including the client, part of collective ownership.
- **No-fuss collaboration — Basecamp**: Use this when competitors add impressive but non-essential features. Writeboard emphasized simple sharing and collaboration.
  - **When to use**: Use it when extra features make the main task harder.
  - **How**: Keep essential features and remove anything that bogs users down.
- **Tell a different story — Seth Godin**: Use a different story when a competitor already owns the customer’s explanation of the category. Do not persuade customers to admit that their current choice was wrong.
  - **When to use**: Use it when direct comparison would place the product on the competitor’s strongest axis.
  - **How**: Choose a different value story. If the competitor is faster, be cheaper. If the competitor sells health, sell convenience. Make the story more important than the one customers already believe.
- **Key problem focus — Michael Reining**: Use the key problem question when competitor comparisons produce feature spreadsheets and distract from the product’s purpose.
  - **When to use**: Use it during product decisions and competitive research.
  - **How**: Ask, “What is the key problem we are trying to solve?” Then ask how the product can solve that problem instead of matching the list.

## Key Concepts
- **Enemy**: A competing product or approach that clarifies what your product rejects.
- **Anti-Project**: Basecamp’s contrast to Microsoft Project’s charts, reports, and command structure.
- **Communication**: The central project-management problem Basecamp chose to solve.
- **Collective ownership**: Shared responsibility for making a project work.
- **No-fuss**: Simple collaboration without non-essential features.
- **Essential feature**: A feature that directly supports the key problem.
- **Different story**: A product explanation that does not repeat the competitor’s narrative.
- **Key problem**: The main user problem that should guide product choices.

## Mental Models
- **Opposition creates boundaries**: Define the rejected approach to make product choices clearer.
- **Story before feature count**: Customers understand a product through the story it tells, not only through a list of functions.
- **Solve the problem, not the competitor**: Use competition as a prompt, then return to the key problem.

## Anti-patterns
- **Following the leader**: Matching the leader’s speed, price, or features keeps the product inside its story.
- **Feature-by-feature imitation**: A spreadsheet can pull the team away from the key problem.
- **Competition obsession**: Overanalyzing other products limits the team’s own vision.
- **Generic positioning**: A simple “we are cheaper” claim may not replace the story customers already believe.
- **Non-essential feature weight**: Extra features make simple collaboration harder and blur the product’s purpose.

## Worked Example
Basecamp saw Microsoft Project as the gorilla in project management and chose to become the anti-Project. The team rejected charts, graphs, reports, statistics, and a project manager broadcasting a plan. It defined the product around communication and collective responsibility, with the client included. The enemy gave Basecamp a product boundary and a clear story without requiring it to imitate Microsoft Project.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name the approach that your product rejects.
2. Turn that contrast into product boundaries and design choices.
3. Focus on communication and shared responsibility when those solve the key problem.
4. Remove features that do not support the main task.
5. Tell a different story instead of competing on the leader’s strongest axis.
6. Use competitor research to clarify your problem, then return to your own vision.
