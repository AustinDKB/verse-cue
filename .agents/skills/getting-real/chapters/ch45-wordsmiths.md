# Chapter 45: Wordsmiths

## Core Idea
When candidates seem close, hire the better writer. Clear writing shows communication, empathy, judgment about what to omit, organized thought, and the ability to make work easy for the reader.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hire good writers — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Treat writing skill as a strong signal for every role.
  - **When to use**: Use it when choosing between similarly qualified designers, programmers, marketers, salespeople, or other candidates.
  - **How**: Compare clarity, concision, editing, organization, and the effort each candidate creates for the reader.
- **An Organized Mind — Dustin J. Mitchell**: Use writing as an indicator of a person’s ability to arrange information and arguments.
  - **When to use**: Use it when you need signals for reliability, professionalism, code quality, or long-distance collaboration.
  - **How**: Read whether the candidate helps other people understand instead of making the reader sort through disorder.
- **Clear Writing Leads To Clear Thinking — Michael A. Covington**: Use expression as a test of understanding.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a person must explain a complex problem or make a decision clear.
  - **How**: Ask the candidate to express the idea and check whether the result makes the reader’s work easy.

## Key Concepts
- **Wordsmith**: A person who uses precise language to make ideas clear.
- **Concise writing**: Writing that communicates the needed idea without extra material.
- **Editing**: The work of removing confusion and unnecessary words.
- **Communication**: The transfer of an idea in a form another person can understand.
- **Empathy**: The ability to think from the reader’s point of view.
- **Omission**: The judgment to leave out information that does not help the reader.
- **Organized mind**: A mind that arranges information and arguments in a useful order.
- **Reader effort**: The work a reader must do to understand a message.

## Mental Models
- **Writing as a thinking test**: If a person cannot express an idea, the person may not understand it yet.
- **Reader-effort rule**: Do what makes the task easy for the reader, not what feels easy for the writer.
- **Cross-domain signal**: Writing quality often carries into code, design, email, messages, professionalism, and reliability.

## Anti-patterns
- **Role-specific discount**: Treating writing as optional for designers or programmers misses its effect on all work.
- **Writer-centered communication**: Doing what feels easy for the writer makes work hard for everyone else.
- **Word volume**: More words do not show more thought when the main idea stays hidden.
- **Unedited output**: Unclear writing spreads confusion into code, messages, and decisions.

## Worked Example
Suppose a team must choose between two candidates with similar technical skill. One candidate explains a design in a short, ordered note and removes details that do not help the reader. The other uses long, unclear messages. Hire the first candidate because the same habits can improve code, design, customer messages, and remote collaboration.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use writing as a tie-breaker across every role.
2. Check clarity, organization, empathy, and omission.
3. Prefer communication that lowers reader effort.
4. Treat clear expression as evidence of clear thinking.
5. Expect writing habits to affect code and reliability.
