# Chapter 82: Publicize Your Screwups

## Core Idea
Tell customers when something goes wrong, even when most customers did not notice. Open, honest reporting gets bad news out of the way, gives customers facts, and creates room for trust. Customers often allow a mistake when they see that the company tells the truth.

The company should report the problem itself. A proactive report prevents the company from entering a defensive position. State what happened, apologize, describe the repair, and explain the steps that should prevent a repeat. Release bad news all at once, but let good news continue in smaller updates so its positive effect lasts.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Publicize Your Screwups — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Make an unexpected failure public instead of hiding it.
  - **When to use**: Use it after downtime, a database problem, a serious slowdown, or another customer-facing failure.
  - **How**: Tell customers what happened, apologize, state that the team fixed the problem, and describe the steps that follow. Report the issue even when only a small group saw it.
- **Swift, Direct, and Honest — Greg Sherwin, Vice President of Application Technology at CNET, and Emily Avila, Principal at Calypso Communications, from A Primer for Crisis PR**: Let the company report bad news before another party reports it.
  - **When to use**: Use it during a crisis or whenever a public failure could place the company on the defensive.
  - **How**: Report the facts quickly and plainly. Do not hide behind spin. Give customers enough information to understand the event and the response.
- **Bad News All at Once, Good News Slowly — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Use different release rhythms for negative and positive information.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team plans a series of public updates about an incident or a product.
  - **How**: Put the complete bad news in the open at once. Spread good news across time to extend the good feeling.

## Key Concepts
- **Screwup**: A company error or product failure.
- **Unexpected downtime**: Product unavailability that the company did not plan.
- **Transparency**: Open communication about what happened and what the team does next.
- **Informed customer**: A customer who has the facts needed to understand an event.
- **Defensive position**: A weakened state in which the company must answer news reported by others.
- **Proactive report**: A company’s own first report about a problem.
- **Database issue**: A technical problem that can cause slowdowns or downtime.
- **Breathing room**: Customer patience granted after honest communication.
- **Crisis PR**: Public communication during a company problem or failure.

## Mental Models
- **Truth creates a trust buffer**: Customers can allow a mistake when the company gives them honest facts.
- **First report, stronger position**: A company that reports its own failure does not wait for another party to define it.
- **Bad news pressure release**: Full disclosure at once prevents repeated surprise and speculation.
- **Good news cadence**: Small positive updates can extend the useful effect of good news.

## Anti-patterns
- **Secret failure**: Hiding a problem treats customers as people who do not deserve facts.
- **Spin**: Indirect or polished language makes the company appear less honest.
- **Defensive silence**: Waiting for customers or outside parties to report the problem weakens the company’s position.
- **Partial bad-news drip**: Releasing negative facts in small pieces prolongs uncertainty and distrust.
- **Blame without repair**: Naming a cause without explaining the fix leaves customers without confidence.

## Worked Example
Basecamp once lost service for several hours during the night. About 99 percent of customers never knew, but the company still posted an “unexpected downtime” notice on its Everything Basecamp blog. The notice explained that database issues caused slowdowns and downtime, apologized, stated that the team fixed the problem, and described steps to prevent a repeat. The company reported the failure because customers deserved to know.

## Key Takeaways
1. Report failures even when most customers did not see them.
2. Let the company give the first clear account of the problem.
3. State the cause, apology, repair, and prevention steps.
4. Use plain facts instead of spin or secrecy.
5. Release bad news completely, then spread good news over time.
6. Use honesty to earn customer patience after a mistake.
