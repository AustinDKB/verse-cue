# Chapter 6: Fund Yourself

## Core Idea
Treat outside money as plan B. Build with the cash, time, and people you have so investor expectations do not replace product quality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Outside money is plan B — Basecamp**: Use this rule when you can start with cash on hand, a small team, or side work. Ask what you can do with three people instead of ten, $20k instead of $100k, three months instead of six, or a day job plus evening work. Remove non-essential work before seeking outside funding.
  - **When to use**: Use it before a funding decision and whenever a plan seems to need more money, people, or time.
  - **How**: Reduce the team, budget, and schedule assumptions. Keep only the work that creates a quality product. Treat investment as a later option, not the starting requirement.
- **Constraints force creativity — Basecamp**: Use limited resources to expose the idea to real users early. A month or two can show whether the idea has promise, can become self-sustainable, or needs a return to the drawing board.
  - **When to use**: Use it when uncertainty is high and a large commitment would make exit difficult.
  - **How**: Build a small version, put it in the wild, judge the response, and continue only when the product earns a reason to continue.
- **Two paths — Jake Walker**: Use the investor-funded versus self-funded comparison when choosing the company’s purpose and pace. Investor money brings higher expectations, salaries, pressure to build for a sale, and pressure to return the initial investment. Self-funding can take more time, cost less, and support growth for its own sake and continued financial benefit.
  - **When to use**: Use it when speed, ownership, quality, and exit goals pull in different directions.
  - **How**: Compare the behavior that each funding path will create, not only the cash it supplies. Consider creative vendor terms as another way to preserve cash.

## Key Concepts
- **Outside money**: Capital from investors that also brings expectations and obligations.
- **Plan B**: A later funding choice, not the default starting point.
- **Cash on hand**: The resources available before an external investment.
- **Constraint**: A limit on time, money, people, or equipment that forces choices.
- **Self-sustainable**: Able to continue without outside cash.
- **Quality over speed**: The choice to take more time for a better product.
- **Creative vendor terms**: Payment arrangements that reduce the need for founder cash.
- **Exit pressure**: The demand to sell or otherwise return investor money.

## Mental Models
- **Funding changes behavior**: Treat capital as a source of expectations, not only a source of cash.
- **Small test before large commitment**: Use early product evidence before accepting obligations that make exit difficult.
- **Constraint as filter**: Let limited resources reveal essential work and remove decorative work.
- **Quality as a long-term asset**: Build a tool that you and customers can live with instead of chasing a quick payout.

## Anti-patterns
- **Funding as the first priority**: It raises expectations before the product proves its value and can shift attention from quality to cashing in.
- **Acting bigger than the company**: Investor expectations can cause a small company to add salaries, staff, and behavior that it does not need.
- **Building for a quick payout**: A quick return is unlikely and the motive shows in the product.
- **Ignoring an early lemon**: A large funded effort makes it harder to return to the drawing board or leave.

## Worked Example
Jake Walker compared Disclive, which used investor money, with The Show, which did not. At Disclive, higher expectations led the team to take salaries, act larger than it was, and focus on building for a sale or another way to repay investors. With The Show, the team used some of its own money and accepted more time to deliver a better product at lower cost. Creative vendor terms reduced the cash need. The company stayed small, self-funded, and focused on growth for its own sake and continued financial benefit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with the resources that you already have.
2. Ask what a smaller team, budget, and schedule can deliver.
3. Test the idea in the wild before accepting investor obligations.
4. Choose quality and durable use over a quick payout.
5. Compare funding paths by the behavior and goals they create.
6. Use creative vendor terms when they preserve independence.
