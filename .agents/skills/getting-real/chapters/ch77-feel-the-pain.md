# Chapter 77: Feel The Pain

## Core Idea
Remove the wall between customer support and product development. Designers and programmers need direct contact with customer voices, complaints, frustrations, and suggestions. Support is not only a service function. It is a direct way to understand the product’s strengths, weaknesses, and missing context.

A team should not outsource that understanding to a call center, a third party, or a layer of statistics. The people who build the application should answer support requests and experience the customer’s problem. When builders feel the pain, they can give better support and make better product decisions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Feel The Pain — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Put product builders on the front line with customers so they can hear and understand the real experience.
  - **When to use**: Use it whenever support, design, and development have become separate groups with little direct contact.
  - **How**: Have the people who build the product read and answer customer messages. Let them hear frustration and suggestions without a buffer. Use that context when designing and changing the application.
- **Cut Out the Middle Man — David Greiner, founder of Campaign Monitor**: Keep support close to development instead of handing customer requests to a junior or separate team.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a request’s reason matters as much as its stated feature need.
  - **How**: Respond personally. Put the builder in the customer’s shoes. Learn why the customer needs something, then use that reason to guide design.
- **Chef and Waiter — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Give product “chefs” direct experience with the customer-facing “front of house.”
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team works in a product kitchen but cannot hear what customers say.
  - **How**: Rotate builders through support or make personal support a normal team duty. Remove the distance between making the product and serving its users.

## Key Concepts
- **Customer support**: Direct help for people who use the application.
- **Development**: The work that builds and changes the application.
- **Design**: The work that shapes the product experience and interface.
- **Kitchen**: The product-building side of the restaurant analogy.
- **Front lines**: The customer-facing place where real problems appear.
- **Buffer**: A person, group, or system that separates builders from customer voices.
- **Customer voice**: Direct words that show what users need, feel, or struggle with.
- **Context**: The reason behind a customer’s stated request.
- **Statistical analysis**: Numerical evidence that can locate trouble but cannot replace direct voices.

## Mental Models
- **Chef as waiter**: Builders understand the meal better when they serve it and hear the customer’s response.
- **Shortest listening path**: Fewer buffers create faster, clearer understanding of customer problems.
- **Context before feature**: Learn why a customer needs something before deciding what to build.
- **Empathy loop**: Direct support creates better help, better understanding, and better product decisions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Outsourced support**: A call center or third party hides customer language and weakens product empathy.
- **Support wall**: Separating support from development prevents builders from hearing the problems they create or can solve.
- **Statistics-only diagnosis**: Numbers can show where trouble exists, but they cannot show what frustration means.
- **Junior filter**: Sending every request to a junior person removes the builder from the customer’s context.
- **Feature-word listening**: Recording only what customers ask for can miss why they need it.

## Worked Example
At Basecamp, the people who build the product personally answer support emails. Customers receive an answer from someone who understands the application, while the team hears problems and frustration directly. When customers are frustrated, the builders can honestly say, “I feel your pain.” Campaign Monitor describes a similar setup: almost all development, support, and marketing work stays with two people. They personally answer every request, learn the reason behind each need, and use that context when they design the product.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep builders close to customer support.
2. Do not outsource the customer voice to a call center or third party.
3. Make product builders answer real support requests.
4. Learn why customers need something before choosing a feature response.
5. Use statistics to locate trouble, but use voices to understand it.
6. Treat front-line contact as product work, not as a separate chore.
