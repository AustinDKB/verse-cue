# Chapter 28: Hold the Mayo

## Core Idea
Ask customers what they do not want, not only what they want. Product quality can improve when the team removes obstacles, unused features, and unnecessary choices instead of adding more capability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ask people what they do not want — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Add removal questions to product research.
  - **When to use**: Use it in surveys, interviews, and research that normally asks for missing features or greater usefulness.
  - **How**: Ask which feature people would remove, what they do not use, and what blocks them most. Compare removal evidence with addition requests before changing the product.
- **More is not the answer — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Treat subtraction as a customer service action.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a product feels crowded or when proposed additions increase complexity without solving a broad problem.
  - **How**: Look for the feature, choice, or behavior that creates the largest burden. Remove it when the gain from simplicity exceeds its value.
- **Innovation comes from saying no to 1,000 things — Steve Jobs**: Protect concentration through deliberate rejection.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a team considers new markets, features, or directions at the same time.
  - **How**: Reject options that would move the team onto the wrong track or spread attention too thin. Concentrate on the work that matters most.

## Key Concepts
- **Removal question**: A research question about what users want to lose.
- **Feature burden**: Difficulty or distraction caused by an existing product element.
- **Unused feature**: Capability that does not help a customer's regular work.
- **Obstacle**: A product element that gets in the user's way.
- **Subtraction**: A product improvement made by removing something.
- **Product concentration**: Attention kept on the few things that matter most.
- **Innovation**: Progress that comes from choosing the right work, not adding the most work.
- **Wrong track**: A direction that creates activity while weakening product focus.

## Mental Models
- **Reverse survey**: Learn from the feature customers would remove, not only the feature they request.
- **Mayo test**: Treat an unwanted extra as a question of removal, not a reason to add more.
- **Attention budget**: Every feature consumes customer attention and team attention.
- **No as concentration**: Rejection keeps effort on the important path.

## Anti-patterns
- **Addition-only research**: Ask only what is missing and create a one-way pressure toward more software.
- **Feature worship**: Protect every existing feature even when it blocks customers.
- **More-is-better design**: Measure product value by the number of options.
- **Market drift**: Enter new directions without rejecting work that distracts from the main product.
- **Ignoring non-use**: Treat unused capability as harmless when it adds clutter and support cost.

## Worked Example
A normal survey may ask, “What feature is missing?” or “What would make the product more useful?” A reverse survey adds three questions: “If you could remove one feature, what would it be?” “What do you not use?” and “What gets in your way most?” The answers show where subtraction can help. The team then applies Steve Jobs's rule: reject many directions so it can concentrate on the few important ones. The result may be a smaller product that serves customers better.

## Key Takeaways
1. Add removal questions to every product research cycle.
2. Look for unused features and obstacles before adding features.
3. Treat subtraction as a direct way to help customers.
4. Reject options that spread product attention too far.
5. Measure focus by what the product leaves out.
