# Chapter 16: Ignore Details Early On

## Core Idea
Work from large to small. Early perfection creates stagnation, disagreement, meetings, and delay, while real use exposes the details that deserve attention. Build the broad shape first, make sure it works, and refine it later.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Devil's in the Details, work from large to small — Patrick Lafleur**: Use this sequence when a design or product has not yet established its overall shape.
  - **When to use**: Use it at the start of a drawing, interface, or feature. Use it again when the team wants to polish a small part before the whole works.
  - **How**: Set the proportions of the whole scene. Sketch the largest objects, then smaller objects. Add volume and shading through repeated evaluation. In software, place the basic elements on the page, use them, and refine the details after the structure works.
- **Details reveal themselves through use — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Let actual use show which details need work.
  - **When to use**: Use it when the team debates a small choice before anyone has used the product.
  - **How**: Make a working version first. Watch where people hesitate, what feels missing, and which “potholes” the team keeps hitting. Improve those points after evidence appears.
- **Just do it later — Jason Fried, David Heinemeier Hansson, and Matthew Linderman**: Delay perfection without abandoning it.
  - **When to use**: Use it when a detail matters eventually but does not block current use.
  - **How**: Record the detail, finish the useful structure, and return to the detail after the product has real feedback.

## Key Concepts
- **Large-to-small**: A build order that establishes structure before polish.
- **Detail**: A small visual, wording, code, or measurement choice.
- **Overall shape**: The broad arrangement that makes a product usable.
- **Premature perfection**: Refinement before the product has earned useful feedback.
- **False progress**: Work that feels precise but does not move the product toward use.
- **Iteration**: Repeated evaluation and adjustment after each usable version.
- **Pothole**: A repeated problem discovered through real use.
- **Tonal sketch**: A loose drawing built with light, medium, and dark tones before fine detail.

## Mental Models
- **Structure before polish**: Treat the whole product as the first problem and each pixel as a later problem.
- **Use as a detector**: Let repeated use reveal missing parts and high-value improvements.
- **Three tones first**: Establish the major relationships before adding small distinctions.
- **Later is not never**: Delay a detail when it does not block use, but return when the product gives evidence.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pixel polishing in week one**: Early font, color, or button changes can hide the lack of a working structure.
- **Code-count contests**: Four lines instead of seven can receive attention without improving the user's result.
- **Precision without purpose**: Chasing 90 percent instead of 89 percent can consume time without changing behavior.
- **A full day on one element**: The team loses momentum and may mistake local polish for product progress.
- **Perfection before use**: The team argues from guesses instead of learning from the product in action.

## Worked Example
Patrick Lafleur described drawing classes as a lesson in sequence. A drawing that starts with details will fail because it misses the whole scene. The artist first gets the proportions right, sketches the largest objects, and works down to the smallest. The artist then adds volume with three tones, light, medium, and dark, and reevaluates those tones for each part through multiple iterations. The same sequence applies to software: put the elements on the page, make sure they work, and later adjust the headline size, green shade, or button position.

## Key Takeaways
1. Establish the product's broad shape before polishing details.
2. Build a usable version before debating small choices.
3. Use real use to find the details that deserve attention.
4. Treat repeated problems as evidence for refinement.
5. Delay perfection when it does not block a working product.
